The test of understanding is explaining it simply to someone who was not there. Your friend knows school algebra and powers, has seen the puzzle's two models, and has never heard of a logarithm.

## Where this leads

Logarithms turn up wherever probabilities are multiplied, and wherever quantities span many sizes.

<Foreshadow lesson="M8.1">

How surprised should a model be by an event of probability $p$? The answer, $-\log p$, makes the surprises of independent events add, just as the logarithms of the probabilities of a passage's words added. A language model is trained to make its total surprise at real text as small as it can.

</Foreshadow>

<Foreshadow lesson="M10.3">

Two probabilities stored as logarithms cannot be added by adding their logarithms, since that multiplies them. M10.3 shows how to add them without ever turning them back into numbers so small that they become 0.

</Foreshadow>

<Foreshadow lesson="M11.6">

Drawn with log scales in both directions, the loss of a language model against its size falls close to a straight line. The relationship that line shows is called a *power law*. Reading that line is how a lab decides how large to build its next model.

</Foreshadow>
