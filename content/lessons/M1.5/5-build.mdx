## Multiplying a thousand probabilities

In Python, `**` raises a number to a power, so `0.2 ** 10` is $0.2^{10}$, and a comparison such as `b > a` gives `True` or `False`.

Mathematically, $0.2^n$ is never 0, however large $n$ is. On a computer, A's score is `0.0` from 463 words on, and B's from 814, so two models whose scores differ by a factor of $10^{301}$ come out equal. This is why models work with the logarithms of probabilities rather than with the probabilities themselves.

The rounding shows through. `math.log(1000, 10)` gives `2.9999999999999996`, not 3, because neither natural logarithm in its division is stored exactly, while `math.log10(1000)` gives exactly `3.0`. That is why the tests compared the two only to about nine significant figures.

## Counting digits

A whole number with 3 digits lies from 100 up to, but not including, 1,000, so its logarithm lies from 2 up to, but not including, 3. In the same way, a whole number with $d$ digits has a logarithm from $d - 1$ up to $d$. The function `math.floor(x)` rounds $x$ down to a whole number: `math.floor(2.7)` gives `2`, and `math.floor(3.0)` gives `3`.

Passing tests are not a proof. `digits(10 ** 15 - 1)`, the number written with fifteen 9s, gives 16. Its logarithm falls short of 15 by only about $4 \times 10^{-16}$, while the numbers Python can store near 15 are about $2 \times 10^{-15}$ apart, so `math.log10` gives exactly `15.0`. The formula is exact, but the stored logarithm is not. Python keeps whole numbers exactly, though, so `len(str(n))`, which writes `n` out as text and counts its characters, is right for every whole number that Python will write out. By default it refuses, with a `ValueError`, to write out one of more than 4,300 digits, while `math.log10` takes a whole number of any size.

SymPy, the algebra library first met in M1.1, knows the rules for logarithms, and its function `expand_log` rewrites a logarithm by them. SymPy's `log` is the natural logarithm, but the rules are the same in every base.

SymPy's letters can carry facts about the numbers they stand for: `symbols('x y', positive=True)` makes two letters that stand for positive numbers. SymPy would not use the product rule while it knew nothing about $x$ and $y$, and it was right not to. The proof in Derive began by writing $a$ as $10^{\log_{10} a}$, which needs $a$ to be positive. For negative numbers the rule breaks down: $(-2) \times (-3) = 6$ has a logarithm, but $-2$ and $-3$ have none among the real numbers. A library that used the rule everywhere would sometimes give false answers, and SymPy prefers to give none.
