The puzzle's lead grows by a factor of 2 with every word. Repeated multiplication of this kind is the subject of the whole lesson, so start with how fast it runs.

## Doubling

After one word, B's score is twice A's. After two words it is $2 \times 2 = 4$ times A's, after three words $2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8$ times, and after $n$ words $2^n$ times. Ten words give $2^{10} = 1{,}024$.

Steady change is different. A lead that gains the same amount with every word, which M1.4, on linear functions, draws as a straight line on an ordinary scale, is overtaken by doubling however fast it gains. Even a lead that gains a million with every word is passed at 25 words, 33,554,432 against 25,000,000, and falls further behind after that, because each doubling adds as much as the whole lead so far. That doubling passes every such line is taken on trust here.

## Powers between the whole numbers

M1.2 gave $a^0$ and $a^{-n}$ their meanings by asking what keeps the rule $a^m \times a^n = a^{m + n}$ true. The same question works for fractions.

In the same way, three copies of $10^{1/3}$ must multiply to $10^1$, so $10^{1/3}$ is the positive number whose cube is 10, about 2.15. Two numbers square to 10, about 3.16 and $-3.16$, and the positive one is chosen, as M1.3's square root chose, so that every power of a positive number is positive.

The same reasoning settles $10^{3/10}$: ten copies of it multiply to $10^3 = 1{,}000$. Since $2^{10} = 1{,}024$ is a little more than 1,000, $10^{0.3}$ is a little less than 2, about 1.995. That is why 2 sits just past 0.3 on the line.

Every fraction can be an exponent in this way. For a positive number $b$, $b^{1/n}$ is the positive number whose $n$th power is $b$, and $b^{m/n}$ is $(b^{1/n})^m$. Exponents that are not fractions, such as $\sqrt{2}$, are reached through fractions as close to them as you like: $10^{\sqrt 2}$ lies between $10^{1.41} \approx 25.7$ and $10^{1.42} \approx 26.3$, and closer fractions pin it down further. So $10^x$ has a meaning for every number $x$, and it rises smoothly as $x$ rises.

The rules for exponents that M1.2 proved for whole numbers keep holding for all of these exponents, as long as the number raised to the power is positive. Proving that, and that $10^x$ rises smoothly through every positive number, needs the idea of a limit, which a later course on calculus introduces. This lesson takes both as given, along with the fact that $\sqrt{2}$ is not a fraction.

This is the meaning M1.1 promised. An hour, 3,600 seconds, sat at about the power 3.56 on M1.1's line, because $10^{3.56} = 10^3 \times 10^{0.56}$, and $10^{0.56}$ is about 3.6.

Splitting the exponent into a whole number and a part between 0 and 1 splits the number into a power of ten and a factor between 1 and 10. That is M1.1's scientific notation: $10^{2.6} \approx 4 \times 10^2$.

## Undoing a power

The function $f(x) = 10^x$ takes a power and gives a number. A larger power always gives a larger number, so no two powers share an output, and $f$ is one-to-one. Take its possible outputs to be exactly the numbers it reaches, and it is onto as well, so M1.3 says it has an inverse.

The inverse takes an output of $10^x$ and gives the power of 10 that makes it. It is called the *logarithm* to base 10, written $\log_{10}$. So $\log_{10} 1{,}000 = 3$, because $10^3 = 1{,}000$, and $\log_{10} 2 \approx 0.301$. A number's position on a log scale is its logarithm, which is where the scale gets its name.

So the logarithm takes exactly the positive numbers. Numbers between 0 and 1 have negative logarithms, and the closer a number is to 0, the more negative its logarithm. The puzzle's probabilities, 0.2 and 0.4, are less than 1, so their logarithms are negative.

## The names

<Definition term="Exponential function">

For a positive number $b$, the function $f(x) = b^x$, which has a meaning for every number $x$. Each step of 1 in $x$ multiplies the output by $b$. A quantity of the form $a \times b^x$, with $a$ positive, *grows exponentially* when $b$ is more than 1, and *decays exponentially*, towards 0 but never reaching it, when $b$ is between 0 and 1.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Logarithm">

For a positive base $b$ other than 1, $\log_b x$ is the power of $b$ that gives $x$: $\log_b x = y$ exactly when $b^y = x$. It is the inverse of the function $b^x$, so among the real numbers it exists only for positive $x$. Base 10 counts powers of ten, $\log_{10} 1{,}000 = 3$, and base 2 counts doublings, $\log_2 8 = 3$. Base 1 is left out because every power of 1 is 1.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Log scale">

A scale on which each equal step multiplies by the same factor, so that a number's position is its logarithm. M1.1's line of powers of ten is one. Anything multiplied by the same factor at every step draws a straight line on a log scale.

</Definition>
