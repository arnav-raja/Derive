Explore found, by trying numbers, that multiplying two numbers adds their logarithms. Now prove that it always does, and use the proof to settle the puzzle.

## Why logarithms turn products into sums

$\log_{10}$ is the inverse of $10^x$, so each undoes the other:

- $10^{\log_{10} x} = x$ for every positive number $x$, and
- $\log_{10}(10^{y}) = y$ for every number $y$.

Together with M1.2's rule for multiplying powers, which Explore extended to every exponent, these are enough to prove that $\log_{10}(ab) = \log_{10} a + \log_{10} b$ for every pair of positive numbers $a$ and $b$.

Call this the *product rule*. Dividing works the same way: since $\frac{a}{b} \times b = a$, the product rule gives $\log_{10}\frac{a}{b} + \log_{10} b = \log_{10} a$, so

$$
\log_{10} \frac{a}{b} = \log_{10} a - \log_{10} b.
$$

With $a = 1$ this says $\log_{10}\frac{1}{b} = -\log_{10} b$, because $\log_{10} 1 = 0$. From here on, $T$ stands for $\log_{10} 2$, which is about 0.30103.

The rule holds for every power $y$, not only for whole numbers. Since $a = 10^{L}$, the rule for a power of a power gives $a^{y} = (10^{L})^{y} = 10^{Ly}$, and undoing the power leaves $\log_{10}(a^{y}) = yL$.

Now the puzzle can be settled. A's score is $0.2^{1000}$, so its logarithm is $1{,}000 \times \log_{10} 0.2 \approx 1{,}000 \times (-0.69897) = -698.97$.

## Any base

Base 10 counts powers of ten. Other bases count other factors: base 2 counts doublings, so $\log_2 1{,}024 = 10$, because ten doublings make 1,024. Calculators and Python have base 10 built in, and every other base can be had from it.

</Reveal>

Nothing in those steps needed the base to be 2. It needed only a positive base other than 1, so that its logarithm is not 0. For every such base $b$ and every positive $x$,

$$
\log_b x = \frac{\log_{10} x}{\log_{10} b}.
$$

Nothing needed base 10 underneath either. For any two positive bases $b$ and $c$ other than 1, the same steps give $\log_b x = \dfrac{\log_c x}{\log_c b}$. Changing the base divides every logarithm by the same number, so logarithms to different bases are the same function drawn to different scales.

## A second view: the graph of a logarithm

M1.3 showed that the graph of an inverse is the graph of the function folded over the line $y = x$, because folding carries each point $(a, b)$ to $(b, a)$. The logarithm to base 2 is the inverse of $2^x$, so its graph comes from folding the graph of $2^x$.
