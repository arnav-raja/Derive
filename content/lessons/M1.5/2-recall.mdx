This lesson builds on M1.1's powers of ten, M1.2's rules for exponents and M1.3's inverse functions. Five quick questions check that they are ready. Answer from memory, without a calculator. A miss opens a short re-teach straight away.

<Exercise id="recall-add-exponents" />

<Reteach for="recall-add-exponents">

<Callback lesson="M1.2">

A power counts copies multiplied together. $2^5$ is five 2s multiplied, and $2^3$ is three more, so the product has eight: $2^5 \times 2^3 = 2^{5 + 3} = 2^8$. Multiplying powers of the same number adds the exponents, $a^m \times a^n = a^{m + n}$.

</Callback>

</Reteach>

<Exercise id="recall-power-of-power" />

<Reteach for="recall-power-of-power">

<Callback lesson="M1.2">

$(10^2)^3$ is three copies of $10^2$ multiplied together, $10^2 \times 10^2 \times 10^2$, and adding the exponents gives $10^{2 + 2 + 2} = 10^6$. A power of a power multiplies the exponents: $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$.

</Callback>

</Reteach>

<Exercise id="recall-negative-power" />

<Reteach for="recall-negative-power">

<Callback lesson="M1.1">

A negative power of ten counts divisions by ten: $10^{-3}$ is 1 divided by 10 three times, $\tfrac{1}{10^3} = \tfrac{1}{1{,}000} = 0.001$. M1.2 showed why the rule for adding exponents needs exactly this: $10^{-3} \times 10^{3}$ must be $10^{0} = 1$. In general $a^{-n} = 1/a^n$.

</Callback>

</Reteach>

<Exercise id="recall-inverse" />

<Reteach for="recall-inverse">

<Callback lesson="M1.3">

$f^{-1}(11)$ is the input that $f$ sends to 11. To undo $f$, undo its steps in reverse order: $f$ doubles and then adds 3, so subtract 3 to get 8, then halve to get 4. Check it: $f(4) = 2 \times 4 + 3 = 11$.

</Callback>

</Reteach>

<Exercise id="recall-has-inverse" />

<Reteach for="recall-has-inverse">

<Callback lesson="M1.3">

A function has an inverse exactly when it is one-to-one, so no two inputs share an output, and onto, so every possible output is reached. $x^2$ and $|x|$ both send 2 and $-2$ to the same output, and neither ever gives a negative number, so neither can be undone. $3x - 1$ gives a different output for every input and reaches every number, and its inverse undoes the steps in reverse order: add 1, then divide by 3.

</Callback>

</Reteach>
