## A point's distance and angle

A point can be given by its two coordinates, or by its distance $r$ from the origin and the angle $\theta$ that turns $(1, 0)$ towards it, since the point is $(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$. `math.hypot(x, y)` gives $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, the distance. For the angle, `math.atan2(y, x)` gives the angle of the point $(x, y)$, in radians, from $-\pi$ to $\pi$: note that $y$ comes first. Python also has `math.atan(t)`, which gives an angle whose slope, $\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$, is $t$.

`math.atan(y / x)` cannot do this job. The points $(1, 1)$ and $(-1, -1)$ have the same slope, $\frac{y}{x} = 1$, so a function given only the slope cannot tell them apart, any more than M1.3's square root could tell $3$ from $-3$. As M1.3 narrowed squaring to the numbers 0 or more so that it could be undone, the slope of an angle is narrowed to the angles between $-\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\frac{\pi}{2}$, where each slope belongs to one angle, and `math.atan` undoes it there. And `y / x` fails outright, with a `ZeroDivisionError`, when $x$ is 0. `math.atan2` is given $y$ and $x$ separately, so it can see which quarter of the plane the point is in.

## Break it: the sine of pi

The sine of $\pi$ is 0, since half a turn from $(1, 0)$ reaches $(-1, 0)$.

The point at `math.pi` is the point at the gap, $\pi$ less `math.pi`, mirrored in the second axis, as the point at $150°$ mirrors the point at $30°$, so the two have the same sine. For so small an angle the sine and the angle agree to every digit a float keeps, so `math.sin(math.pi)` measures the gap itself, about $1.22 \times 10^{-16}$: how far `math.pi` is from $\pi$. Floats are compared within a tolerance, as M1.4's tests compared them with `close`, never with `==`.
