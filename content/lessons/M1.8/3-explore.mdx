## Evidence and proof

The puzzle's forty primes were evidence, and $n = 40$ was a counterexample: as M1.7 found, one counterexample breaks a claim about every case. A claim with only a few cases, as a truth table has four rows, is settled by checking them all. A claim about every whole number has infinitely many cases, and to show that it holds in each of them, mathematics uses a *proof*: a chain of statements, each of which follows from a definition, from a result already proved, or from what was assumed, by a rule that can be named. Such a proof reasons about a letter, such as $n$, that stands for any whole number at all, so what it shows holds for each of them at once.

## Starting from definitions

A proof can use only what its words mean, so it starts from definitions. The *integers* are the whole numbers and their negatives: $\dots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots$. An integer $n$ is *even* when $n = 2k$ for some integer $k$, and *odd* when $n = 2k + 1$ for some integer $k$. So 6 is even, with $k = 3$, and $-7$ is odd, with $k = -4$. Dividing an integer by 2 leaves a remainder of 0 or 1, so every integer is even or odd, and none is both.

Here is a claim: the sum of any two odd integers is even. Four arguments are offered for it.

1. $1 + 3 = 4$ and $5 + 7 = 12$, and a computer checked every pair of odd integers below a million: each sum was even.
2. Write the two odd integers as $2k + 1$ and $2k + 1$. Their sum is $4k + 2 = 2(2k + 1)$, which is even.
3. Write them as $2j + 1$ and $2k + 1$, for integers $j$ and $k$. Their sum is $2j + 2k + 2 = 2(j + k + 1)$, and $j + k + 1$ is an integer, so the sum is even.
4. An odd integer plus an odd integer is always even, so the sum of these two is even.

Only the third covers every pair. The first checks many pairs, and a million pairs are not every pair. The second uses one letter for both numbers, so it proves the claim only for an odd integer added to itself: two odd integers chosen separately, such as 1 and 3, need letters of their own. The fourth assumes what it set out to prove, and so is no argument at all.

The third is a *direct proof*. It assumes the first part of an if-then, here that $a$ and $b$ are odd, writes out what that means by the definitions, and works forward, one justified step at a time, to the second part, that $a + b$ is even. Its letters $j$ and $k$ stand for any integers, so it covers every pair at once.

## Cases

The puzzle's formula gave forty odd numbers, from 41 to 1,601. Is $n^2 + n + 41$ odd for every integer $n$?

$n^2 + n = n(n + 1)$ is a product of two neighbouring integers, and one of them is even. Saying why takes two cases, which between them cover every integer. If $n$ is even, $n = 2k$, and $n(n + 1) = 2k(n + 1)$ is even. If $n$ is odd, $n + 1$ is even, say $n + 1 = 2k$, and $n(n + 1) = 2kn$ is even. Either way $n^2 + n$ is even, and adding $41 = 2 \times 20 + 1$ makes it odd. A *proof by cases* splits a claim into cases that together cover everything, and proves each one.

Adding 41 alone settles nothing, since $n^2 + n$ might have been odd. And when $n$ is odd, $n^2$ is odd too, as the Derive phase proves, so $n^2 + n$ is odd plus odd, which is even by the last section, and adding 41 makes it odd again. So the formula never gives an even number. In fact no prime below 41 divides any of its values, which is why its primes last so long. That is stated here without proof, because its proof needs number theory beyond this course.

## Turning a claim round

Here is a claim about every integer $n$: if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even. A direct proof starts from $n^2 = 2k$ and soon stalls, at $n = \sqrt{2k}$, which says nothing about whether $n$ is even. M1.7 found another way in: $P \Rightarrow Q$ and its contrapositive, $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$, say exactly the same, so a proof of either one proves both.

</Reveal>

<Reveal for="explore-which-to-prove">

The contrapositive denies both parts and swaps them. Not "$n$ is even" is "$n$ is odd", and not "$n^2$ is even" is "$n^2$ is odd", so the claim says the same as "if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd". That version starts from $n = 2k + 1$, something concrete to work with, and the Derive phase finishes it. The converse, "if $n$ is even, then $n^2$ is even", is true, but it is a different claim. "If $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd" denies both parts without swapping them, which says the same as the converse, not the claim. And one example proves nothing about every integer. Proving $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ in place of $P \Rightarrow Q$ is a *proof by the contrapositive*.

## Assuming the opposite

Some claims have no if to assume, such as "$\sqrt{2}$ is not a fraction". A number is *rational* when it can be written as a fraction $\frac{p}{q}$ of integers, with $q \neq 0$, and *irrational* when it cannot. Every fraction can be put in *lowest terms*, where no whole number greater than 1 divides both $p$ and $q$: divide both by any number that does, which keeps the fraction's value and makes $q$ smaller in size, and repeat, which must stop, since $q$ cannot shrink for ever. In lowest terms, in particular, $p$ and $q$ are not both even.

A *proof by contradiction* assumes that the claim is false and reasons from there until it reaches something impossible: a statement and its denial, both true. Here is the reasoning for $\sqrt{2}$, as far as the point where something goes wrong. It uses the claim of the last section, if $n^2$ is even then $n$ is even, whose proof the Derive phase completes.

Suppose that $\sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q}$, with $p$ and $q$ integers, $q \neq 0$, and the fraction in lowest terms, so that $p$ and $q$ are not both even. Squaring both sides gives $2 = \frac{p^2}{q^2}$, so $p^2 = 2q^2$, and $p^2$ is even. By the claim of the last section, $p$ is even, say $p = 2r$. Then $4r^2 = 2q^2$, so $q^2 = 2r^2$, and $q^2$ is even, so $q$ is even too. So $p$ and $q$ are both even.

The steps end with $p$ and $q$ both even, while the supposition said that they are not both even: a statement and its denial, both true, which is impossible. Every step after the supposition was valid, so the supposition itself must be false, and no fraction equals $\sqrt{2}$: it is irrational. Squaring can add solutions to an equation, as M1.2 found, but here the reasoning runs one way only: if $\sqrt{2} = \frac{p}{q}$, then $p^2 = 2q^2$ must hold, and that is all the proof needs.

To prove a claim $C$ by contradiction, assume $\neg C$, reason until some statement and its denial are both true, and conclude $C$. The irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$ was known in ancient Greece, and the Derive phase justifies each step of its proof.

## Infinitely many primes

Euclid, around 300 BC, proved that the primes never run out. Here is his argument, told as a proof by contradiction. Suppose that there were only finitely many primes, and that $2, 3, 5, \dots, P$ is the complete list. Multiply them all together and add 1:

$$
N = 2 \times 3 \times 5 \times \dots \times P + 1.
$$

Dividing $N$ by any prime on the list leaves a remainder of 1, so none of them divides $N$. But $N$ is greater than 1, so it has a prime factor: its smallest factor greater than 1 is prime, since otherwise it would have a factor greater than 1 and smaller than itself, which would be a smaller factor of $N$. That prime is not on the list, so the list was not complete after all. The supposition is false, and there are infinitely many primes.

Now take the first six primes, which are not the complete list, and make the same number from them: $N = 2 \times 3 \times 5 \times 7 \times 11 \times 13 + 1 = 30{,}031$.

$30{,}031 = 59 \times 509$. None of the six primes divides it, as the proof says, and its prime factors, 59 and 509, are primes missing from the list, as the proof also says. But nothing in the proof said that $N$ is prime, only that its prime factors are not on the list. Reading more into a proof than it proves is as easy as reading too much into forty cases. ## Arguments that fail

Some arguments look like proofs and are not. The commonest proves the converse. A team reasons like this about a model:

> If a model has memorised its training examples, its loss on them is far below its loss on new data. Our model's training loss is far below its test loss. So our model has memorised its training examples.

</Reveal>

<Reveal for="explore-converse-argument">

From $P \Rightarrow Q$ and $Q$, nothing follows about $P$: that would need the converse, $Q \Rightarrow P$, which the team never had. A test set unlike the training data, or a bug in the code that works out the test loss, gives the same gap with no memorising at all. The valid form runs the other way, by the contrapositive: from $P \Rightarrow Q$ and $\neg Q$, conclude $\neg P$. Had the training loss not been far below the test loss, the team could have concluded that the model had not memorised its examples. Whether the first sentence is true is a separate question: an argument is valid when its conclusion follows from its premises, true or not.

The second common failure divides by 0. Here is a "proof" that $2 = 1$. Let $a$ and $b$ be equal numbers, neither of them 0.

1. $a = b$.
2. $a^2 = ab$, multiplying both sides by $a$.
3. $a^2 - b^2 = ab - b^2$, taking $b^2$ from both sides.
4. $(a + b)(a - b) = b(a - b)$, factorising both sides.
5. $a + b = b$, dividing both sides by $a - b$.
6. $2b = b$, since $a = b$.
7. $2 = 1$, dividing both sides by $b$.

Since $a = b$, $a - b$ is 0, and step 5 divides by 0. Step 4 is true, but only because both sides are 0: $(a + b) \times 0 = b \times 0$ holds whatever $a + b$ and $b$ are, so it cannot show that they are equal. As M1.2 found, dividing both sides of an equation by an expression is safe only where the expression is not 0. Step 7 divides by $b$, which is allowed, since $b$ is not 0. A third failure is circular: the fourth argument for the odd sum assumed the very claim it set out to prove.

## The names

<Definition term="Proof">

A *proof* of a claim is a chain of statements, each following from definitions, from results already proved or from what is assumed, by a rule that can be named, and ending with the claim. A letter that stands for any case lets one proof cover every case at once. Checking cases settles a claim only when every case is checked, as in a truth table. For a claim about infinitely many cases, agreeing cases are evidence, not proof, while one counterexample disproves it. An argument fails when it proves the converse, divides by something that may be 0, or assumes what it set out to prove.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Direct proof and proof by cases">

A *direct proof* of "if $P$ then $Q$" assumes $P$ and works forward to $Q$, usually by writing out definitions: the *integers* are the whole numbers and their negatives, and an integer $n$ is *even* when $n = 2k$ and *odd* when $n = 2k + 1$, for some integer $k$. A *proof by cases* splits a claim into cases that together cover everything, such as $n$ even and $n$ odd, and proves each one.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Proof by the contrapositive">

To prove $P \Rightarrow Q$, it is enough to prove $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$, which says the same. It helps when $\neg Q$ gives more to start from than $P$ does, as "$n$ is odd" gives $n = 2k + 1$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Proof by contradiction">

To prove a claim, assume that it is false and reason until some statement and its denial are both true. The assumption must then be false, so the claim is true. A number is *rational* when it can be written as a fraction $\frac{p}{q}$ of integers with $q \neq 0$, and *irrational* when it cannot: $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational, and there are infinitely many primes, both proved by contradiction.

</Definition>
