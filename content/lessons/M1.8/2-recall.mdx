This lesson builds on M1.7's statements, implications and quantifiers, and on M1.2's algebra. Five quick questions check that they are ready. Answer from memory, without a calculator. A miss opens a short re-teach straight away.

<Exercise id="recall-false-if" />

<Reteach for="recall-false-if">

<Callback lesson="M1.7">

"If $P$ then $Q$" is a promise about the cases where $P$ holds. It is broken only when $P$ is true and $Q$ is false. When $P$ is false, nothing can break it, so it is true, vacuously: "if an email is labelled spam, then it contains a link" says nothing about an email that is not labelled spam.

</Callback>

</Reteach>

<Exercise id="recall-contrapositive" />

<Reteach for="recall-contrapositive">

<Callback lesson="M1.7">

The *contrapositive* of $P \Rightarrow Q$ is $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$: deny both parts and swap them. It says exactly the same as the original, since both are false only when $P$ is true and $Q$ is false. The *converse*, $Q \Rightarrow P$, swaps the parts without denying them, and need not say the same.

</Callback>

</Reteach>

<Exercise id="recall-deny-every" />

<Reteach for="recall-deny-every">

<Callback lesson="M1.7">

"For every $x$, $P(x)$" is denied by a single exception: not for every $x$, $P(x)$, is there is an $x$ with not $P(x)$. So "every run converged" is false as soon as one run did not, and its denial is "some run did not converge", not "no run converged".

</Callback>

</Reteach>

<Exercise id="recall-de-morgan" />

<Reteach for="recall-de-morgan">

<Callback lesson="M1.7">

De Morgan's laws carry a not inside a bracket, and on its way and and or swap: not ($P$ and $Q$) is (not $P$) or (not $Q$). "Accurate and fast" fails as soon as a model is inaccurate, or slow, or both.

</Callback>

</Reteach>

<Exercise id="recall-expand" />

<Reteach for="recall-expand">

<Callback lesson="M1.2">

Multiplying out a bracket multiplies every term of one by every term of the other: $(a + b)^2 = (a + b)(a + b) = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$. The middle term, $2ab$, is the one most often forgotten. A check at one value catches a slip: at $a = b = 1$, both sides are 4.

</Callback>

</Reteach>
