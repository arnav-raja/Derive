## Choosing, exactly

$\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n(n - 1) \cdots (n - k + 1)}{k!}$ has $k$ factors on top and $k$ below, so the count can be built up a pair of factors at a time, one from the top and one from below. Start from $\binom{n}{0} = 1$. After $i$ passes of a loop, keep the running value equal to $\binom{n}{i}$, the first $i$ factors on top over $i!$; then one more pass brings in the next factor on top and the next one below. Multiply first and then divide, and every division comes out exact, since its answer is a count, $\binom{n}{i + 1}$, a whole number. That is a loop invariant, as in E1.2. Python's `//` divides one whole number by another and gives a whole number, an `int`, which is exact however large it is, as E1.1 showed.

Choosing $k$ is choosing the $n - k$ left out, so when $k$ is more than half of $n$, $\binom{n}{n - k}$ gives the same count with a shorter loop. Python's own version is `math.comb(n, k)`.

Dividing first would go wrong: $\binom{5}{2}$ would reach $5 \mathbin{//} 2 \times 4 = 8$, not 10, since 5 is not a multiple of 2, while $5 \times 4$ is. So would `count *= (n - i) // (i + 1)`, which works out $(n - i) \mathbin{//} (i + 1)$ first: it gets $\binom{5}{2}$ right by luck, but gives $\binom{4}{2}$ as $4 \times (3 \mathbin{//} 2) = 4$. The largest number the loop ever holds is $k$ times the count, just before its last division, where the factorial formula builds $n!$: for $\binom{1{,}000}{3}$, a number with 9 digits against one with 2,568.

## The triangle, row by row

Pascal's rule builds each row of the triangle from the row above it: each entry inside the row is the sum of the two entries above it, and each row starts and ends with 1. ## Every choice, listed

Pascal's rule can list the sets, not only count them. A set of $k$ of the items either holds the first item, with $k - 1$ more chosen from the rest, or leaves it out, with all $k$ chosen from the rest. That is the split of the Explore phase, by the first item instead of the last, and it makes `choices` a recursive function, as in E1.5: it calls itself on the rest of the list, which is shorter. The base cases answer the smallest questions: how many ways are there to choose nothing, and how many to choose more items than there are? Each answer is a list of sets, as every answer of `choices` is. Python's own version is `itertools.combinations`, and the tests compare yours with it.

The calls of `choices` follow Pascal's rule exactly: the call for $k$ of $n$ items makes one call for $k - 1$ of $n - 1$ and one for $k$ of $n - 1$, so the lists it returns have $\binom{n - 1}{k - 1} + \binom{n - 1}{k} = \binom{n}{k}$ sets between them, and an induction on the length of the list proves that it lists each set exactly once. Listing costs far more than counting: `choose(30, 15)` finds $\binom{30}{15} = 155{,}117{,}520$ in 15 passes of its loop, while a list of that many sets of 15 features would need more than 10 gigabytes of memory.

## Every podium

`arrangements(items, k)` should list every ordered choice of $k$ of the items, which it calls an arrangement, such as every podium of 3 from 10 models. It fills the first place with each item in turn, and calls itself to fill the other places from the items left, as E1.5's functions called themselves on smaller lists.
