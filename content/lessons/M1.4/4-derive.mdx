## Two points fix a line

A linear function is $f(x) = mx + c$, so two numbers decide it, and two points are enough to find them. The slope is the rise between the points divided by the step; then $c$ is whatever makes the line pass through one of them.

Here is a rule you used in M1.3 without being told where it came from. Water freezes at 0 °C, which is 32 °F, and boils at 100 °C, which is 212 °F, and the two scales are related by a steady rule.

<Steps>
  <Step why="the two points, as (Celsius, Fahrenheit)">$(0, 32)$ and $(100, 212)$</Step>
  <Step why="the slope is the rise divided by the step, in degrees Fahrenheit per degree Celsius">$m = \dfrac{212 - 32}{100 - 0} = 1.8$</Step>
  <Step why="at C = 0 the rule gives c, and the first point says that is 32">$c = 32$</Step>
  <Step why="the line through both points">$F = 1.8C + 32$</Step>
</Steps>

The slope carries units, as every rate does: each degree Celsius is 1.8 degrees Fahrenheit.

The slope is $(13 - 5) \div (6 - 2) = 2$ hours per billion tokens: each extra billion tokens costs 2 more hours. Then $5 = 2 \times 2 + c$ gives $c = 1$: an hour of start-up, spent before any training happens, which is the time the rule gives at $n = 0$. So $T = 2n + 1$, and the check at the other point works: $2 \times 6 + 1 = 13$.

## The rate of any quadratic

The same cancelling works for every quadratic.

</Reveal>

<Reveal for="derive-general-rate">

The rise is $f(b) - f(a) = 3(b^2 - a^2) - 2(b - a)$, because the 5s cancel. Both parts have the factor $b - a$, so the rise is $(b - a)\big(3(b + a) - 2\big)$, and dividing by $b - a$ leaves $3(a + b) - 2$. The same steps on $f(x) = px^2 + qx + r$ give the average rate of change from $a$ to $b$ as

$$
p(a + b) + q.
$$

The number $r$ drops out, because adding a number lifts the whole curve without changing any rise. Two of Explore's findings follow from this formula.

The rises of rises. With steps of 1, the rise from $x$ to $x + 1$ is the rate times the step, $p(2x + 1) + q$. Moving $x$ on by 1 adds $2p$ to that rise. So the rises of the rises are all $2p$: 2 for $x^2$, and 6 for $3x^2 + x$, as the table showed.

Where the chord sits. The sum $a + b$ is twice the midpoint of $a$ and $b$, so the rate depends only on where the middle of the interval is, not on how wide it is. For $x^2$, the pairs 1 and 3, and 0 and 4, share the midpoint 2, and both have the rate 4.

## Completing the square

Explore rewrote $x^2 - 6x + 11$ as $(x - 3)^2 + 2$, which shows its bottom at once. Here is how to find such a rewriting for any quadratic. A squared bracket multiplies out as $(x + h)^2 = x^2 + 2hx + h^2$, so the number in the bracket is half the number in front of $x$. Take $x^2 + 8x + 20$:

<Steps>
  <Step why="half of 8 is 4, and (x + 4)² multiplies out to x² + 8x + 16; split 20 as 16 + 4">$x^2 + 8x + 20 = (x^2 + 8x + 16) + 4$</Step>
  <Step why="the bracket is a square">$= (x + 4)^2 + 4$</Step>
</Steps>

The squared bracket is never negative and is 0 only at $x = -4$, so the lowest value is 4, at $x = -4$.

Half of $-10$ is $-5$, and $(x - 5)^2 = x^2 - 10x + 25$. Since $31 = 25 + 6$, the quadratic is $(x - 5)^2 + 6$, and its vertex is $(5, 6)$.

The steps never used the particular numbers, so they work with letters as well.

</Reveal>

<Reveal for="derive-general-square">

$x^2 + bx + c = \big(x + \tfrac{b}{2}\big)^2 + c - \tfrac{b^2}{4}$, so the bottom of $x^2 + bx + c$ is at $x = -\tfrac{b}{2}$. When the number in front of $x^2$ is some $a$ other than 1, take it out of the first two terms first:

<Steps>
  <Step why="a is not 0, so it can be taken out of the first two terms">$ax^2 + bx + c = a\Big(x^2 + \dfrac{b}{a}x\Big) + c$</Step>
  <Step why="complete the square inside, with b/a in place of b">$= a\Big(\Big(x + \dfrac{b}{2a}\Big)^2 - \dfrac{b^2}{4a^2}\Big) + c$</Step>
  <Step why="multiply out the outer bracket">$= a\Big(x + \dfrac{b}{2a}\Big)^2 + c - \dfrac{b^2}{4a}$</Step>
</Steps>

When $a > 0$ the squared part is never negative, so the lowest value, $c - \dfrac{b^2}{4a}$, comes where the bracket is 0. When $a < 0$ the same point is the highest. Either way the vertex is at

$$
x = -\frac{b}{2a}.
$$

## A second angle: the mirror

Explore found the bottom of $x^2 - 6x + 11$ another way, halfway between two inputs of equal height, and that works in general too. The first two terms, $ax^2 + bx = x(ax + b)$, are 0 at $x = 0$ and at $x = -\tfrac{b}{a}$. So $ax^2 + bx + c$ has the same height, $c$, at both, and the vertex is halfway between them, at $x = -\tfrac{b}{2a}$. One argument used algebra and the other symmetry, and they agree.

## Where a quadratic is zero

Completing the square also solves $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$.

<Steps>
  <Step why="the completed square, set equal to 0">$a\Big(x + \dfrac{b}{2a}\Big)^2 + c - \dfrac{b^2}{4a} = 0$</Step>
  <Step why="move the number part to the other side, then divide both sides by a">$\Big(x + \dfrac{b}{2a}\Big)^2 = \dfrac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2}$</Step>
  <Step why="when the right side is positive, two numbers have it as their square, as M1.3 found; the ± takes both, and also covers the sign of 2a">$x + \dfrac{b}{2a} = \pm\dfrac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$</Step>
  <Step why="subtract b/(2a) from both sides">$x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$</Step>
</Steps>

This is the *quadratic formula*. The number $b^2 - 4ac$ decides how many solutions there are. When it is positive there are two, mirrored about the vertex. When it is 0 there is one, the vertex itself. When it is negative there are none, because no number has a negative square.

## Why the mean wins

Now the puzzle. Its squared error is a quadratic in the guess $w$, so completing the square shows its lowest point.

<Exercise id="derive-mean-best" />

</Reveal>

<Reveal for="derive-mean-best">

$E(w) = 3(w - 4)^2 + 14$. The squared part is never negative and is 0 only at $w = 4$, so the smallest squared error is 14, at $w = 4$, the mean, exactly as the puzzle found.

Now take any three values $y_1$, $y_2$ and $y_3$. Each square multiplies out as $(y - w)^2 = y^2 - 2yw + w^2$, and adding the three gives

$$
E(w) = 3w^2 - 2(y_1 + y_2 + y_3)\,w + \big(y_1^2 + y_2^2 + y_3^2\big).
$$

That is a quadratic in $w$ with $a = 3$ and $b = -2(y_1 + y_2 + y_3)$, so its vertex is at

$$
w = -\frac{b}{2a} = \frac{2(y_1 + y_2 + y_3)}{6} = \frac{y_1 + y_2 + y_3}{3},
$$

the mean. With $n$ values instead of three, the same steps give $nw^2$ and $-2 \times (\text{their sum}) \times w$, and the vertex is the sum divided by $n$: the mean again. So, under squared error, the best single guess is the mean, for any data at all.
