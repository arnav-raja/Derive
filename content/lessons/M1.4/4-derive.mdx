## Two points fix a line

A linear function is $f(x) = mx + c$, so two numbers decide it, and two points with different $x$ are enough to find them. The slope is the rise between the points divided by the step; then $c$ is whatever makes the line pass through one of them.

Here is a rule you used in M1.3, on functions and inverses, without being told where it came from. Water freezes at 0 °C, which is 32 °F, and boils at 100 °C, which is 212 °F, and the two scales are related by a steady rule.

<Steps>
  <Step why="the two points, as (Celsius, Fahrenheit)">$(0, 32)$ and $(100, 212)$</Step>
  <Step why="the slope is the rise divided by the step, in degrees Fahrenheit per degree Celsius">$m = \dfrac{212 - 32}{100 - 0} = 1.8$</Step>
  <Step why="at C = 0 the rule gives c, and the first point says that is 32">$c = 32$</Step>
  <Step why="the line through both points">$F = 1.8C + 32$</Step>
</Steps>

The slope carries units, as every rate does: a rise of 1 °C is a rise of 1.8 °F.

## The rate of any quadratic

The same cancelling works for every quadratic.

The same steps on $f(x) = px^2 + qx + r$ give the average rate of change from $u$ to $v$ as

$$
p(u + v) + q.
$$

The number $r$ drops out, because adding a number lifts the whole curve without changing any rise. Two of Explore's findings follow from this formula.

The rises of rises. With steps of 1, the rise from $x$ to $x + 1$ is the rate times the step, $p(2x + 1) + q$. Moving $x$ on by 1 adds $2p$ to that rise. So the rises of the rises are all $2p$: 2 for $x^2$, and 6 for $3x^2 + x$, as the table showed.

Where the chord sits. The sum $u + v$ is twice the midpoint of $u$ and $v$, so the rate depends only on where the middle of the interval is, not on how wide it is. For $x^2$, the pairs 1 and 3, and 0 and 4, share the midpoint 2, and both have the rate 4.

## Completing the square

Explore rewrote $x^2 - 6x + 11$ as $(x - 3)^2 + 2$, which shows its bottom at once. Here is how to find such a rewriting for any quadratic. A squared bracket multiplies out as $(x + h)^2 = x^2 + 2hx + h^2$, so the number in the bracket is half the number in front of $x$. Take $x^2 + 8x + 20$:

<Steps>
  <Step why="half of 8 is 4, and (x + 4)² multiplies out to x² + 8x + 16; split 20 as 16 + 4">$x^2 + 8x + 20 = (x^2 + 8x + 16) + 4$</Step>
  <Step why="the bracket is a square">$= (x + 4)^2 + 4$</Step>
</Steps>

The squared bracket is never negative and is 0 only at $x = -4$, so the lowest value is 4, at $x = -4$.

So the bottom of $x^2 + bx + c$ is at $x = -\tfrac{b}{2}$. When the number in front of $x^2$ is some $a$ other than 1, take it out of the first two terms first:

<Steps>
  <Step why="a is not 0, so it can be taken out of the first two terms">$ax^2 + bx + c = a\Big(x^2 + \dfrac{b}{a}x\Big) + c$</Step>
  <Step why="complete the square inside, with b/a in place of b">$= a\Big(\Big(x + \dfrac{b}{2a}\Big)^2 - \dfrac{b^2}{4a^2}\Big) + c$</Step>
  <Step why="multiply out the outer bracket">$= a\Big(x + \dfrac{b}{2a}\Big)^2 + c - \dfrac{b^2}{4a}$</Step>
</Steps>

When $a > 0$ the squared part is never negative, so the lowest value, $c - \dfrac{b^2}{4a}$, comes where the bracket is 0. When $a < 0$ the same point is the highest. Either way the vertex is at

$$
x = -\frac{b}{2a}.
$$

## A second angle: equal heights

The rate of a quadratic gives the vertex without completing the square. For $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$, the chord from $u$ to $v$ has rate $a(u + v) + b$, so $f(v) - f(u) = (v - u)\big(a(u + v) + b\big)$. Two different inputs therefore have the same height exactly when $a(u + v) + b = 0$, that is, when their midpoint $\tfrac{u + v}{2}$ is $-\tfrac{b}{2a}$. So inputs of equal height come in pairs mirrored about the line $x = -\tfrac{b}{2a}$, as Explore saw for one bowl. When $a > 0$, every chord to the right of that line slopes up and every chord to its left slopes down, so the bottom is on the line. Completing the square and the chords are different arguments, and they agree.

## Where a quadratic is zero

Completing the square also solves $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$.

<Steps>
  <Step why="the completed square, set equal to 0">$a\Big(x + \dfrac{b}{2a}\Big)^2 + c - \dfrac{b^2}{4a} = 0$</Step>
  <Step why="move the number part to the other side and divide both sides by a; then c/a is 4ac/(4a²), so the right side is one fraction over 4a²">$\Big(x + \dfrac{b}{2a}\Big)^2 = \dfrac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2}$</Step>
  <Step why="when the right side is positive, two numbers have it as their square, as M1.3 found when it undid squaring, and the ± takes both; the square root of 4a² is 2a when a > 0 and −2a when a < 0, and the ± covers that sign too">$x + \dfrac{b}{2a} = \pm\dfrac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$</Step>
  <Step why="subtract b/(2a) from both sides, and write the result as one fraction">$x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$</Step>
</Steps>

This is the *quadratic formula*. The number $b^2 - 4ac$ decides how many solutions there are. When it is positive there are two, mirrored about the vertex. When it is 0 there is one, the vertex itself. When it is negative there are no real solutions, because no real number has a negative square; M1.12, on complex numbers, meets numbers that do.

## Why the mean wins

Now the puzzle. Its squared error is a quadratic in the guess $w$, so completing the square shows its lowest point.

<Exercise id="derive-mean-best" />

</Reveal>

<Reveal for="derive-mean-best">

In $E(w) = 3(w - 4)^2 + 14$ the squared part is never negative and is 0 only at $w = 4$, so the smallest squared error is 14, at $w = 4$, the mean, exactly as the puzzle found.

Now take any three values $y_1$, $y_2$ and $y_3$. Each square multiplies out as $(y - w)^2 = y^2 - 2yw + w^2$, and adding the three gives

$$
E(w) = 3w^2 - 2Sw + Q, \quad \text{where } S = y_1 + y_2 + y_3 \text{ and } Q = y_1^2 + y_2^2 + y_3^2.
$$

For the puzzle's waiting times, $S = 12$ and $Q = 62$.

<Exercise id="derive-mean-general" />

</Reveal>

<Reveal for="derive-mean-general">

The number in front of the squared bracket is 3, which is positive, so the squared part is never negative and is 0 only at $w = \tfrac{S}{3}$: the sum of the three values divided by 3, which is their mean. So for any three values, the mean gives the smallest squared error. With $k$ values instead of three, the same steps give $kw^2 - 2Sw + Q = k\big(w - \tfrac{S}{k}\big)^2 + Q - \tfrac{S^2}{k}$, and the best guess is $\tfrac{S}{k}$, the mean again. So, under squared error, the best single guess is the mean, for any data at all.
