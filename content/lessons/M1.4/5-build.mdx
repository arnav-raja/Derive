Derive found formulas for lines, vertices and solutions. Now build them in Python, where tests can try each one on many cases at once.

## A line from two points

In M1.3 a Python function took one input. It can take several, separated by commas:

```python
def area(width, height):
    return width * height

print(area(3, 4))   # prints 12
```

<Exercise id="build-line" />

<Reveal for="build-line">

A function can call another function, as `intercept` calls `slope` here. Naming the points in the other order changes both the rise and the step into their negatives, and their ratio stays the same.

## A formula with a bug

Python's maths library holds the square root. The line `import math` loads it, and then `math.sqrt(9)` gives `3.0`. A function can also give back two values at once, separated by a comma, and Python prints them in brackets, such as `(1.0, 2.0)`.

</Reveal>

<Reveal for="build-roots-bug">

Python works through `*` and `/` from left to right, which is why `/ 2*a` multiplies by `a` after dividing by 2. With $a = 1$ the two agree, which is why the first equation came out right and hid the bug. This is the slip M1.2's expression trees were built to catch: the tree for `/ 2*a` has the multiplication at the top.

## Break it: a different score

The puzzle scored a guess by its squared error. Here is that score in Python, with the totals for three guesses.

</Reveal>

<Reveal for="build-absolute-error">

Adding the sizes of the misses is called the *absolute error*, and its best single guess is the middle value, the *median*. Moving the guess a little towards the middle value shortens more misses than it lengthens, until the guess reaches the middle. Its curve is made of straight pieces, not a smooth bowl, so completing the square cannot find its bottom.

The median barely notices a wild value: for 1, 2, 3 and 94 it is 2.5, however large the 94 is. Which score to train with is a real choice in machine learning, and squared error, with its smooth bowl and its mean, is the most common.

## Compare with the library

SymPy, the algebra library from M1.2 and M1.3, can solve a quadratic with letters left in, so you can compare its answer with the formula you derived.

<Reveal for="build-sympy-quadratic">

SymPy prints `formula` as

```text
[(-b - sqrt(-4*a*c + b**2))/(2*a), (-b + sqrt(-4*a*c + b**2))/(2*a)]
```

which is the quadratic formula, with $b^2 - 4ac$ written in another order. SymPy did the algebra itself, so its answer holds for every $a$, $b$ and $c$, not just a few, and the tests checked it by putting each solution back into the equation. For `doubled` it gives `[1, 2]`, the same answer as the fixed `roots`.
