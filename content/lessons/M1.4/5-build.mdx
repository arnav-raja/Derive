
## Break it: a different score

The puzzle scored a guess by its squared error. Here is that score in Python, with the totals for three guesses.

<Exercise id="build-absolute-error" />

</Reveal>

<Reveal for="build-absolute-error">

Adding the sizes of the misses is called the *absolute error*, and its best single guess is the middle value, the *median*. Moving the guess a little towards the middle value shortens more misses than it lengthens, until the guess reaches the middle. (With an even number of values, every guess between the two middle ones gives the same total; the median, halfway between them, is one of these best guesses.) Its curve is made of straight pieces, not a smooth bowl, so completing the square cannot find its bottom.

The median barely notices a wild value: for 1, 2, 3 and 94 it is 2.5, however large the 94 is, and every guess from 2 to 3 gives the same total, 94. Which score to train with is a real choice in machine learning, and squared error, with its smooth bowl and its mean, is the most common.

## Compare with the library

SymPy, which checked equivalences in M1.2 and found inverses in M1.3, can solve a quadratic with letters left in, so you can compare its answer with the formula you derived.

<Exercise id="build-sympy-quadratic" />

<Reveal for="build-sympy-quadratic">

SymPy prints `formula` as

```text
[(-b - sqrt(-4*a*c + b**2))/(2*a), (-b + sqrt(-4*a*c + b**2))/(2*a)]
```

which is the quadratic formula, with $b^2 - 4ac$ written in another order. SymPy did the algebra itself, so its answer holds for every $b$ and $c$ and every $a$ except 0, not just a few values; when $b^2 - 4ac$ is negative the square root is not a real number, and there are no real solutions, as Derive found. The tests checked it by putting each solution back into the equation, and `doubled`, `[1, 2]`, agrees with the fixed `roots`.
