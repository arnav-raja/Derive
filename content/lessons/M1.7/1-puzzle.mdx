A team building a spam filter labels emails by hand, and its labelling guide makes a claim about the data:

> If an email is labelled spam, then it contains a link.

Before training on the data, you want to check the claim. Each email's record is a card with its label on one side and, on the other, whether the email contains a link. Four cards lie on the table, and you can see one side of each:

| Card | The side you can see |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Labelled spam |
| 2 | Labelled not spam |
| 3 | Contains a link |
| 4 | Contains no link |

<Reveal for="puzzle-which-cards">

When the psychologist Peter Wason set puzzles of this shape in the 1960s, fewer than one person in ten chose the right cards. Most chose the cards that could agree with the claim, and passed over the one that could break it. Checking data, a proof or a program the same way, by looking only for agreement, is the easiest way to fool yourself.

This lesson builds the words and symbols for saying exactly what a claim says, and exactly what would make it false: not, and, or and if-then, sets and their pictures, and "for every" and "there is".
