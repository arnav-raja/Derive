## Statements

A *statement* is a sentence that is either true or false, such as "this email contains a link" or "$7 < 3$". Small words build new statements out of old ones. *Not* turns a statement round: "not $P$" is true exactly when $P$ is false. *And* joins two statements: "$P$ and $Q$" is true only when both are true.

A filter that tidies a dataset keeps or drops each email by a rule. Here is one:

> Keep an email if it is short or it is in English, and drop every other email.

<Exercise id="explore-or" />

<Reveal for="explore-or">

In mathematics, and in programming languages, *or* means "one or the other or both". Everyday speech sometimes uses it to mean "one or the other but not both", as in "tea or coffee?"; in mathematics that meaning is said in full, or called *exclusive or*.

A table can list every case at once. With T for true and F for false, here are the three words:

| $P$ | $Q$ | not $P$ | $P$ and $Q$ | $P$ or $Q$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T | T | F | T | T |
| T | F | F | F | T |
| F | T | T | F | T |
| F | F | T | F | F |

Each row is a case, and the four rows are every case there is, so two statements built from $P$ and $Q$ say the same thing exactly when their columns agree in every row. Such statements are called *equivalent*, just as M1.2's expressions were when they agreed for every value of their letters, and a single row where they differ shows that they are not.

## Denying an and

A second filter is stricter:

> Keep an email if it is short and in English, and drop every other email.

It drops every email for which "short and in English" is false: every email for which "not (short and in English)" is true.

</Reveal>

<Reveal for="explore-drop">

An email fails "short and in English" as soon as one part fails, so not ($P$ and $Q$) says the same as (not $P$) or (not $Q$), and their two columns of the truth table agree in every row:

| $P$ | $Q$ | not ($P$ and $Q$) | (not $P$) or (not $Q$) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T | T | F | F |
| T | F | T | T |
| F | T | T | T |
| F | F | T | T |

The other way round, "not ($P$ or $Q$)" says that neither is true, which is (not $P$) and (not $Q$). These are *De Morgan's laws*, after the nineteenth-century mathematician Augustus De Morgan:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{not}\,(P \text{ and } Q) &= (\text{not } P) \text{ or } (\text{not } Q), \\
\text{not}\,(P \text{ or } Q) &= (\text{not } P) \text{ and } (\text{not } Q).
\end{aligned}
$$

The not passes inside the brackets, and on its way and and or swap.

## Sets

The emails that a rule keeps form a *set*: a collection of things, called its *elements*. $x \in A$ says that $x$ is an element of $A$, and $x \notin A$ that it is not. A set can be listed in braces, as $\{2, 3, 5, 7\}$ is, or described by a rule, as in $\{x : x^2 = 9\}$, read "the set of $x$ such that $x^2 = 9$". That set is $\{-3, 3\}$, the solutions of the recall's equation, and in the same way $\{x : x \text{ is a short email}\}$ is the set of short emails. The order in which a set's elements are written does not matter, and an element is either in a set or not, never in it twice: $\{3, -3\}$ and $\{-3, 3, 3\}$ are both the set $\{-3, 3\}$. The set with no elements at all is the *empty set*, $\varnothing$.

Each of the three words has a partner for sets. Write $U$ for the set of all the emails, $S$ for the short ones and $E$ for the ones in English:

- the *union* $S \cup E$ is the set of emails that are short or in English;
- the *intersection* $S \cap E$ is the set of emails that are short and in English;
- the *complement* $S^c$ is the set of emails in $U$ that are not short.

A picture with a circle for each set, a *Venn diagram*, shows every case at once: each region is one row of the truth table. Choose regions to shade them, in the picture or in the list under it, and check your shading against each set the figure asks for.

## If, then

The labelling guide's claim joins two statements with *if* and *then*: "if an email is labelled spam, then it contains a link". With $P$ for "labelled spam" and $Q$ for "contains a link", it is written $P \Rightarrow Q$ and read "$P$ implies $Q$". Another claim of the same shape comes from a lab's rules for its machines: "if a job runs for more than a day, then it must be approved".

<Exercise id="explore-break-rule" />

</Reveal>

<Reveal for="explore-break-rule">

So $P \Rightarrow Q$ is false in one case only, when $P$ is true and $Q$ is false, and true in the other three:

| $P$ | $Q$ | $P \Rightarrow Q$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| T | T | T |
| T | F | F |
| F | T | T |
| F | F | T |

The last two rows surprise most people at first. The labelling guide's claim is a promise about the emails labelled spam, and an email that is not labelled spam cannot break it, so the claim holds for that email, though it says nothing about it. A statement that is true because its "if" part is false is said to be true *vacuously*.

A claim can be reworded in many ways, and not every rewording keeps its meaning.

<Exercise id="explore-same-claim" />

</Reveal>

<Reveal for="explore-same-claim">

Two rewordings have names. $Q \Rightarrow P$ swaps the two parts and is called the *converse*: "if an email contains a link, it is labelled spam" makes a different promise, which a newsletter with a link would break. $\text{not } Q \Rightarrow \text{not } P$ denies both parts and swaps them, and is called the *contrapositive*: "if an email contains no link, it is not labelled spam". The contrapositive always says the same as the original, since both are false in exactly one case, a spam email with no link. When both $P \Rightarrow Q$ and $Q \Rightarrow P$ are claimed, the claim is written $P \Leftrightarrow Q$ and read "$P$ if and only if $Q$".

In the language of sets, write $P$ also for the set of emails labelled spam and $Q$ for the set of emails with a link, the elements for which each statement is true. The claim says that no email is in $P$ and outside $Q$: $P \cap Q^c = \varnothing$. Then every element of $P$ is an element of $Q$, which is written $P \subseteq Q$, "$P$ is a *subset* of $Q$". The figure asks instead for the set of emails that the claim allows: shade it.

## For every, and there is

The claim is about every email in the data: for every email $x$, if $x$ is labelled spam then $x$ contains a link. A sentence with a letter in it, such as "$x$ is labelled spam", is a *predicate*, written $P(x)$. Like one of M1.3's functions, it gives each element an output, here true or false, and it becomes a statement once $x$ is chosen or once it is said how many $x$ it is claimed for. "For every" is written $\forall$, an upside-down A, and "there is", meaning at least one, is written $\exists$, a backwards E:

$$
\forall x \in U : P(x) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \exists x \in U : P(x)
$$

say that $P(x)$ is true for every element of $U$, and for at least one. Read them "for every $x$ in $U$, $P(x)$" and "there is an $x$ in $U$ with $P(x)$". A quantifier covers everything after its colon, so in $\forall x \in U : P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x)$ the "for every" reaches the whole implication. They are called *quantifiers*, because they say how many of the elements a statement is claimed for. The puzzle's claim, in symbols, is $\forall x \in U : P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x)$.

</Reveal>

<Reveal for="explore-not-every">

"Not every" means "at least one not": not ($\forall x : P(x)$) is $\exists x : \text{not } P(x)$. In the same way, not ($\exists x : P(x)$) is $\forall x : \text{not } P(x)$, since there is no $x$ with $P(x)$ exactly when every $x$ fails it. So a single *counterexample* is enough to break a for-every claim, as the pair $a = b = 1$ broke the claimed identity in the recall. A single *witness* is enough to prove a there-is claim, but no number of examples proves a for-every claim about infinitely many cases.

"Every model trains well at some learning rate" is $\forall m\ \exists r : W(m, r)$, with $W(m, r)$ for "model $m$ trains well at rate $r$". The rate is chosen after the model, so each model may have a rate of its own. "Some learning rate trains every model well" is $\exists r\ \forall m : W(m, r)$: one rate, chosen first, must suit every model. The second promises more. If one rate suits every model, then every model has a rate, that one, so $\exists r\ \forall m$ always carries $\forall m\ \exists r$ with it, but not the other way round, as the grid shows. M1.3's onto has the first shape: a function $f$ is onto when for every $y$ in the codomain there is an input $x$ with $f(x) = y$, $\forall y\ \exists x : f(x) = y$, and each $y$ may have an input of its own.

The figure now shows which statements hold, with the cells that prove each one or break it, and its cells can be changed. Try to make "Some learning rate trains every model well" true while "Every model trains well at some learning rate" is false, and see what stops you. Real training behaves like the grid: the rate that trains a network well usually shrinks as the network grows, so a rate tuned on a small model rarely suits a large one.

## The names

<Definition term="Not, and and or">

A *statement* is true or false. Not $P$, written $\neg P$, is true when $P$ is false. $P$ and $Q$, written $P \land Q$, is true when both are. $P$ or $Q$, written $P \lor Q$, is true when at least one is, both included. Two statements are *equivalent* when they agree in every row of their *truth table*, and *De Morgan's laws* say that $\neg(P \land Q)$ is $\neg P \lor \neg Q$ and $\neg(P \lor Q)$ is $\neg P \land \neg Q$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="If and then">

$P \Rightarrow Q$, "if $P$ then $Q$", is false only when $P$ is true and $Q$ is false. Its *contrapositive* $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ is equivalent to it, and its *converse* $Q \Rightarrow P$ need not be. $P \Leftrightarrow Q$, "$P$ if and only if $Q$", claims both $P \Rightarrow Q$ and $Q \Rightarrow P$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Sets">

A *set* is a collection of *elements*, and $x \in A$ says that $x$ is one of them. The *union* $A \cup B$, the *intersection* $A \cap B$ and the *complement* $A^c$ are the sets for or, and and not. $A \subseteq B$, "$A$ is a *subset* of $B$", says that every element of $A$ is in $B$, $\varnothing$ is the *empty set*, and $|A|$ is the number of elements of a finite set $A$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Quantifiers">

A *predicate* $P(x)$ is true or false for each $x$. $\forall x : P(x)$ says that it is true for every $x$, and $\exists x : P(x)$ that it is true for at least one. $\neg\,\forall x : P(x)$ is $\exists x : \neg P(x)$, and $\neg\,\exists x : P(x)$ is $\forall x : \neg P(x)$. The order of two quantifiers matters: $\exists y\ \forall x$ claims more than $\forall x\ \exists y$.

</Definition>
