This is the first lesson, so there is no earlier Derive lesson to call back to. It builds instead on two pieces of school arithmetic: converting between units of time, and powers of ten. Four quick questions check that both are ready to use. Answer from memory, without a calculator. A miss is useful here: it opens a short re-teach straight away.

## Units of time and speed

<Exercise id="recall-seconds-hour" />

<Reteach for="recall-seconds-hour">

There are 60 seconds in a minute and 60 minutes in an hour, so an hour is $60 \times 60 = 3600$ seconds. Each conversion multiplies by how many of the smaller unit fit into one of the larger. A day is then $24 \times 3600 = 86{,}400$ seconds.

</Reteach>

<Exercise id="recall-speed" />

<Reteach for="recall-speed">

Average speed is distance divided by time: $150 \text{ km} \div 2 \text{ h} = 75$ kilometres in each hour. Look at the unit as well as the number. Kilometres divided by hours gives "kilometres per hour", written km/h. That habit of dividing the units along with the numbers is the first idea of this lesson.

</Reteach>

## Powers of ten

<Exercise id="recall-powers-multiply" />

<Reteach for="recall-powers-multiply">

$10^3$ means three tens multiplied together: $10 \times 10 \times 10 = 1000$. So $10^3 \times 10^4$ is three tens multiplied by four more tens, which is seven tens multiplied together, $10^7$. Multiplying powers of ten adds the exponents: $10^a \times 10^b = 10^{a+b}$.

</Reteach>

<Exercise id="recall-small-power" />

<Reteach for="recall-small-power">

Each step down in the exponent divides by ten:

$$
10^2 = 100, \quad 10^1 = 10, \quad 10^0 = 1, \quad 10^{-1} = 0.1, \quad 10^{-2} = 0.01, \quad 10^{-3} = 0.001.
$$

A negative exponent means "one divided by": $10^{-3} = 1/10^3$. It is not a negative number. $10^{-3}$ is small but still positive.

</Reteach>
