## The puzzle, derived from its units

You have three quantities:

- $\sym{C}{C}$, the compute, in FLOP;
- $\sym{R}{R}$, the chip's speed, in FLOP per second;
- $\sym{Y}{Y}$, the number of seconds in a year, in seconds per year.

<Exercise id="derive-time-formula" />

<Reveal for="derive-time-formula">

Dividing $C$ by $R$ leaves seconds, and dividing seconds by seconds per year leaves years:

$$
T = \frac{C}{R\,Y}, \qquad \frac{\text{FLOP}}{\dfrac{\text{FLOP}}{\text{s}} \times \dfrac{\text{s}}{\text{year}}} = \text{year}.
$$

Try any other arrangement of the three and the units come out wrong. The units did not merely check the formula; they chose it from among the ways of arranging $C$, $R$ and $Y$. What they cannot choose is a pure number in front, as you will see later in this phase.

Every rule in that chain is a rule of ordinary algebra. Nothing special was needed for units: FLOP and s behaved exactly like the letters $a$ and $b$ would. That is the whole content of "units as algebra", and it is why a calculation that keeps its units checks itself as it goes.

## A second angle: in your head

A result worth trusting should survive being reached another way. This time, ask first how much one chip does in a year, then how many such chip-years GPT-3 needs. Use rough numbers and no calculator. A useful fact: a year is very nearly $\pi \times 10^7$ seconds, so about $3 \times 10^7$.

<Steps>
  <Step why="a year is about π × 10⁷ s">$1\ \text{year} \approx 3 \times 10^{7}\ \text{s}$</Step>
  <Step why="one chip's work in a year: its speed times the seconds in a year">$10^{14}\ \text{FLOP/s} \times 3 \times 10^{7}\ \text{s/year}$</Step>
  <Step why="multiply the leading numbers and add the exponents; the seconds cancel">$= 3 \times 10^{21}\ \text{FLOP/year}$</Step>
  <Step why="divide the leading numbers and subtract the exponents; the FLOP cancel">$\dfrac{3 \times 10^{23}\ \text{FLOP}}{3 \times 10^{21}\ \text{FLOP/year}} = 10^{2}\ \text{years}$</Step>
</Steps>

One chip does about $3 \times 10^{21}$ FLOP in a year, and GPT-3 needs about a hundred of those chip-years: about a century again, by a route you can carry in your head. Both routes rest on the same formula, $T = C/(RY)$, so their agreement checks the arithmetic, not the formula. The units checked the formula.

## Finding a formula you did not know

So far the units have checked formulas, or chosen between a few. They can also find a formula from the list of quantities it depends on.

A pendulum is a weight on a string, swinging back and forth. Its period $T$ is the time for one full swing, out and back again, in seconds. It could depend on three things: the string's length $L$ in metres, the strength of gravity $g$ in metres per second squared (m/s²), and the mass $M$ of the weight in kilograms. Only one way of combining them gives seconds.

The units cannot see a pure number in front, such as 2 or π, so look for the period only up to such a number. The sign $\propto$, read "is proportional to", says exactly that: $T \propto x$ means that $T$ is $x$ times some pure number. Find the combination in two steps.

<Exercise id="derive-pendulum-square" />

<Reveal for="derive-pendulum-square">

A square root undoes a square: $\sqrt{9} = 3$, because $3^2 = 9$.

<Exercise id="derive-pendulum-period" />

</Reveal>

<Reveal for="derive-pendulum-period">

Two things fall out that are easy to miss.

First, the mass cannot appear at all. Nothing else in the problem carries kilograms to cancel it, so a heavy weight and a light one on the same string swing with the same period. Experiments confirm it.

Second, the units cannot give the whole answer. The full physics gives $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$, and the $2\pi$ is a pure number with no unit, invisible to dimensional analysis. The angle the swing starts from is a pure number too, so the units cannot say how it matters either: the formula holds for small swings, and wide ones take a little longer. So dimensional analysis can rule out a formula whose units do not match, and can find how a quantity scales, but it cannot prove a formula right.

</Reveal>

## Estimates with honest bounds

Fermi estimates often start from bounds. You may not know how many words a person reads a minute, but you are sure it is more than 100 and less than 1,000. Which single number should you then guess?

<Exercise id="derive-geomean" />

<Reveal for="derive-geomean">

The guess $x = \sqrt{ab}$ is called the *geometric mean* of the bounds. It is the right middle when your uncertainty is about factors. For the reading speed, between 100 and 1,000 words a minute, it is $\sqrt{100 \times 1000} = \sqrt{100{,}000} \approx 316$, so guess about 300.

Compare it with the ordinary average, $(a + b)/2$, for bounds of 100 and 1,000,000:

The ordinary average sits a factor of 2 below the upper bound and a factor of 5,000 above the lower one. The geometric mean sits a factor of 100 from each. With powers of ten it is quick to find: average the exponents. Between $10^2$ and $10^6$ the geometric mean is $10^4$.
