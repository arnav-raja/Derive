In 2020, OpenAI described a language model called GPT-3. It has 175 billion adjustable numbers, called *parameters*, and it learnt from about 300 billion *tokens* of text. A token is a short piece of text: often a whole word, sometimes part of one.

Training means working through all that text and nudging every parameter a little at a time. All of that work comes down to simple arithmetic on numbers, one addition or one multiplication at a time. Each of these is a *floating-point operation*, written FLOP.

<Stated where="L4.8">

Training a model like GPT-3 takes about 6 FLOP for each parameter and each token of text. So the total compute is $\sym{C}{C} \approx 6\,\sym{N}{N}\,\sym{D}{D}$, where $N$ is the number of parameters and $D$ is the number of tokens.

</Stated>

For GPT-3, with $N$ = 175 billion and $D$ = 300 billion, that comes to about 315 followed by 21 zeros. Written more compactly,

$$
C \approx 3.15 \times 10^{23} \text{ FLOP},
$$

where $10^{23}$ means a 1 followed by 23 zeros.

Now picture a single chip working through those operations without a break. It manages $10^{14}$ FLOP every second, a hundred million million. That is roughly what a good data-centre accelerator of 2020 sustained in real use.

<Reveal for="puzzle-one-chip">

Instinct is a poor guide with numbers like these, in either direction, because nothing in daily life comes in $10^{23}$ of anything. That is not a failing. It is the reason the tools in this lesson exist:

- units that multiply, divide and cancel like symbols, so the right formula falls out of them;
- powers of ten, so that the arithmetic takes seconds and can be done in your head;
- estimates that are honest about how far off they might be.

By the end of the Derive phase you will derive "about a century" yourself from the units, check it in your head, and work out how many chips the lab would have needed to finish in a month instead.

</Reveal>
