The puzzle's answer, about a century, is roughly $3 \times 10^9$ seconds. Is that a lot of seconds? Seconds suit a heartbeat and suit a century badly. To see why, put several spans of time on one line.

## Arithmetic with powers of ten

In the recall phase, $10^3 \times 10^4 = 10^7$: multiplying powers of ten adds the exponents, because it counts how many tens are multiplied together. Division runs the other way.

<Exercise id="explore-divide" />

<Reveal for="explore-divide">

Write $10^{23}$ as 23 tens multiplied together and $10^{14}$ as 14 tens. Dividing cancels 14 of the tens on top against the 14 underneath, and 9 are left:

$$
\frac{3.15 \times 10^{23}}{10^{14}} = 3.15 \times 10^{23-14} = 3.15 \times 10^{9}.
$$

Dividing powers of ten subtracts the exponents. The leading number, 3.15, comes along unchanged. A few digits times a power of ten keeps a number's size in plain sight: the exponent says roughly how big it is, and the leading digits say the rest.

</Reveal>

## The names

You have used four ideas in this phase. Here are their names.

<Definition term="Units as algebra">

A quantity is a number times a unit, such as $3.15 \times 10^9$ s. Units multiply, divide and cancel exactly as symbols do in algebra, so FLOP ÷ (FLOP/s) = s.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Powers of ten">

Writing a number as a few digits times a power of ten, such as $3.15 \times 10^{23}$, is called *scientific notation*. The power of ten gives the number's *order of magnitude*, its size to the nearest factor of ten or so. Multiplying adds exponents and dividing subtracts them.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Dimensional analysis">

Checking a formula by checking that its units come out right. If the units are wrong, the formula is wrong. In the Derive phase you will see that it can also find formulas.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Fermi estimation">

Estimating a quantity by breaking it into factors you can guess or look up, and judging the answer by the factor it might be off by.

</Definition>
