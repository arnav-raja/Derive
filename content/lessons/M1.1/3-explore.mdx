The puzzle's answer, about a century, comes from dividing one enormous number by another and keeping track of what the result is measured in. This phase builds the tools for both, starting with the arithmetic.

## Arithmetic with powers of ten

In the Recall phase, $10^3 \times 10^4 = 10^7$: multiplying powers of ten adds the exponents, because it counts how many tens are multiplied together. Division runs the other way.

<Exercise id="explore-divide" />

<Reveal for="explore-divide">

Write $10^{23}$ as 23 tens multiplied together and $10^{14}$ as 14 tens. Dividing cancels 14 of the tens on top against the 14 underneath, and 9 are left:

$$
\frac{3.15 \times 10^{23}}{10^{14}} = 3.15 \times 10^{23-14} = 3.15 \times 10^{9}.
$$

Dividing powers of ten subtracts the exponents. The leading number, 3.15, comes along unchanged. Written this way, a number keeps its size in plain sight: the exponent says roughly how big it is, and the leading digits say the rest.

Both units come from treating FLOP and s exactly like symbols in algebra. Dividing by a fraction multiplies by its upside-down version, and then the FLOP cancel:

$$
\text{FLOP} \div \frac{\text{FLOP}}{\text{s}} = \text{FLOP} \times \frac{\text{s}}{\text{FLOP}} = \text{s}.
$$

Multiplying leaves nothing to cancel, so the FLOP pile up instead:

<ExpressionTree
  title="The same quantities, multiplied"
  description="A tree for C times R, which multiplies the compute in FLOP by the speed in FLOP per second."
  expr="C*R"
  values={{ C: 3.15e23, R: 1e14 }}
  units={{ C: "FLOP", R: "FLOP/s" }}
/>

The number at the top, about $3 \times 10^{37}$, means nothing, and the unit says so before you even look at the number: FLOP squared per second cannot be a length of time. So the formula "time = compute × speed" is wrong, and you can tell without doing any arithmetic.

Units also convert. A year averages 365.25 days, because every fourth year has an extra day, and a day has $24 \times 60 \times 60 = 86{,}400$ seconds. So the number of seconds in a year, $\sym{Y}{Y}$, is

$$
Y = 365.25 \times 86{,}400 = 31{,}557{,}600 \ \text{s/year} \approx 3.16 \times 10^{7}\ \text{s/year}.
$$

Since 1 year and 31,557,600 s are the same span of time, the fraction $\dfrac{1\ \text{year}}{31{,}557{,}600\ \text{s}}$ is equal to 1. Multiplying a quantity by it changes the unit and not the quantity: the seconds cancel and years are left. That is all converting a unit means, and the Derive phase uses it to turn the chip's seconds into years.

Adding is stricter still. You can multiply any two units, even if the result is meaningless, but you can only add quantities of the same kind. Three hours plus twenty minutes is fine once both are in minutes. Three hours plus twenty kilograms is not a quantity at all. Here is an attempt to add seconds to seconds per year:

## The names

You have used four ideas in this phase. Here are their names.

<Definition term="Units as algebra">

A quantity is a number times a unit, such as $3.15 \times 10^9$ s. Units multiply, divide and cancel exactly as symbols do in algebra, so FLOP ÷ (FLOP/s) = s. Converting a unit means multiplying by a fraction equal to 1, such as $\dfrac{1\ \text{year}}{31{,}557{,}600\ \text{s}}$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Powers of ten">

Writing a number as a number from 1 up to, but not including, 10, times a power of ten, such as $3.15 \times 10^{23}$, is called *scientific notation*. The power of ten gives the number's *order of magnitude*, its size to the nearest factor of ten or so. Multiplying adds exponents and dividing subtracts them.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Dimensional analysis">

Checking a formula by checking that its units come out right. If the units do not match, the formula is wrong. In the Derive phase you will see that the units can also find formulas.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Fermi estimation">

Estimating a quantity by breaking it into factors you can guess or look up, and judging the answer by the factor it might be off by.

</Definition>
