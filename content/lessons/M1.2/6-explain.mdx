The test of understanding is explaining it simply to someone who was not there. Your friend is clever, remembers school algebra as a list of moves, and has just seen the puzzle's `False`.

## Where this leads

The ideas from this lesson return whenever a computer does algebra, and whenever a proof is needed.

<Foreshadow lesson="E1.1">

Python reads every expression as exactly these trees and works it out from the leaves up, so you can ask the computer about the order of operations directly. You will also meet $x = x + 1$ again, as an instruction rather than an impossible equation.

</Foreshadow>

<Foreshadow lesson="M13.2">

Rounding after every operation is how computers really calculate. M13.2 measures how much error that adds, and which calculations make it large, such as subtracting two numbers that are nearly equal.

</Foreshadow>

<Foreshadow lesson="M1.8">

The proofs that $a \times 0 = 0$ and $(-1)(-1) = 1$ were direct proofs: a chain of rules from what you know to what you want. M1.8 makes such proofs a habit, and adds proof by contradiction.

</Foreshadow>
