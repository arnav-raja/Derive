School algebra comes as a set of moves: swap the order of a sum, take out a common factor, move a term to the other side. This lesson asks why those moves are allowed. Here is a reason to care.

A computer adds 0.1, 0.2 and 0.3 in two different groupings. First it adds 0.1 and 0.2, then adds 0.3 to the result. Then it starts again, adds 0.2 and 0.3 first, and adds 0.1 to that. Ordinary arithmetic says both come to 0.6.

In Python, which M1.1 used as a careful calculator, this line asks whether the two results are equal:

```python
(0.1 + 0.2) + 0.3 == 0.1 + (0.2 + 0.3)
```

The double equals sign `==` compares the two sides and answers `True` if they are equal and `False` if they are not.

<Exercise id="puzzle-grouping" />

<Reveal for="puzzle-grouping">

Python answers `False`. It shows the left side as 0.6000000000000001 and the right side as 0.6.

Nothing is broken. A computer keeps only about 16 significant figures of each number, and it rounds after every single operation. It cannot even store 0.1 exactly; a later lesson on how computers store numbers, M10.1, explains why. So the two groupings round different intermediate results, and the roundings land on neighbouring values.

A rule you have used since school, that the grouping of a sum does not change it, fails for the computer's numbers. That raises the questions this lesson answers. What exactly are the rules of algebra? Why do they hold for ordinary numbers? Which of them survive rounding, and why? By the end of the Build phase you will break that rule yourself, on purpose, with a calculator that keeps only three figures, and you will find out whether rounding can break its neighbour, $a + b = b + a$, as well.
