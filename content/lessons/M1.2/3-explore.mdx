Algebra is a language, and like any language it has a grammar. Before asking why its rules hold, look at what an expression really is.

## An expression is a tree

When you write $3 + 4 \times 2$, you mean: multiply 4 by 2, then add 3. The order of operations is a convention, but once it is agreed, every expression has exactly one shape. Draw that shape as a tree. Numbers sit at the bottom as leaves, each operation joins the values beneath it, and the value of the whole expression appears at the top.

Step through it. Evaluating an expression means working up from the leaves, one operation at a time, which is exactly what a computer does. Brackets change the shape, and with it the value:

<ExpressionTree

Same numbers, same operations, different tree, different value. Reading an expression means finding its tree.

Because a sum can be regrouped, $2 + 3 + 4$ can be written without brackets: both trees give 9. A chain of divisions is different: $16 \div 4 \div 2$ is 2 but $16 \div (4 \div 2)$ is 8, so there the brackets carry meaning.

The order of two numbers is a separate question with the same answer. Swapping them never changes a sum or a product, $a + b = b + a$ and $a \times b = b \times a$, while $8 - 3$ and $3 - 8$ differ, and so do $8 \div 2$ and $2 \div 8$.

## Multiplying out a bracket

In the Recall phase you worked out $7 \times 98$. The quick way splits 98 as $100 - 2$ and works out $7 \times 100 - 7 \times 2$ instead. The two expressions have different trees but the same value:

Multiplying out a bracket turns one multiplication into two and moves the subtraction to the top. This is the only rule that connects addition with multiplication, so every step that turns a product into a sum, or a sum into a product, uses it.

## A calculator that rounds

Now the puzzle. A computer keeps about 16 significant figures and rounds after every operation. A calculator that keeps only three makes the same effect easy to see.

Keeping three significant figures means keeping the first three digits, counted from the first digit that is not 0, and rounding away the rest: 2.718 becomes 2.72, 25.64 becomes 25.6 and 0.0012345 becomes 0.00123. In a whole number, zeros at the end only fill places, so 1,000 needs one significant figure and 2,500 needs two.

Computed exactly, both sums are 1.008. In the first grouping each 0.004 is added to 1 on its own, and each time the rounding throws it away. In the second, the two small amounts are added first, and 0.008 is large enough to survive: 1.008 rounds to 1.01. The grouping rule holds for exact numbers. A calculator that rounds breaks it, and that is all the puzzle was.

## The names

Here are the names of the rules you have just found, with the rules for 0 and 1, and for negatives and reciprocals, which Derive needs.

<Definition term="Expression tree">

The shape of an expression: leaves are numbers or letters, and each operation combines the values beneath it. The order of operations and brackets decide the shape, and evaluation works up from the leaves.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Commutativity">

The order of two numbers does not matter: $a + b = b + a$ and $ab = ba$. It holds for addition and multiplication, not for subtraction or division.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Associativity">

The grouping of a chain does not matter: $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$ and $(ab)c = a(bc)$. Again it holds for addition and multiplication only.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Distributivity">

Multiplication spreads over addition: $a(b + c) = ab + ac$. It is the one rule that links the two operations. It covers subtraction too, because $b - c$ is $b + (-c)$, so $a(b - c) = ab - ac$.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Identities and inverses">

Adding 0 and multiplying by 1 change nothing: $a + 0 = a$ and $a \times 1 = a$. Every number $a$ has a negative with $a + (-a) = 0$, and every number except 0 has a reciprocal with $a \times \tfrac{1}{a} = 1$.

</Definition>

A number system that obeys all of these rules, and in which 0 and 1 are different numbers, is called a *field*, and the rules are the *field axioms*. The ordinary numbers form a field. A computer's rounded numbers do not, quite, as the puzzle showed.
