The Explore phase watched complex numbers multiply their lengths and add their angles, and watched $\left(1 + \frac{i\pi}{n}\right)^n$ come to $-1$. Now prove both: that multiplying by $\cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ turns the plane, that the angle-addition formulas follow in one line, how to divide, where the solutions of $z^n = 1$ lie, and why $\left(1 + \frac{i\theta}{n}\right)^n$ ends on the circle at angle $\theta$.

## Multiplying is turning

So multiplying by $r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$ turns through $\theta$ and then stretches every length by $r$. Every complex number is $r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$ for its own length $r$ and angle $\theta$, so multiplying by it multiplies lengths by $r$ and adds $\theta$ to angles: what the Explore phase saw, now for every pair of numbers. In particular $z^n$, which is $n$ copies of $z$ multiplied together, has length $r^n$ and angle $n\theta$.

## Two turns in one line

## Why the steps end on the circle

The Explore phase watched $\left(1 + \frac{i\theta}{n}\right)^n$ end near the point at angle $\theta$ on the circle of radius 1. Each factor $1 + \frac{i\theta}{n}$ is the point $\left(1, \frac{\theta}{n}\right)$, a short step straight up from 1, or straight down for a negative $\theta$, and multiplying by it turns through its angle and stretches by its length. Its length is $\sqrt{1 + \frac{\theta^2}{n^2}}$, by Pythagoras' theorem, a hair over 1. So the point of the circle of radius 1 in the same direction is only a hair lower, at a height of nearly $\frac{\theta}{n}$, and for a small angle M1.11 found that height nearly equal to the angle itself: their ratio comes closer and closer to 1 as the angle shrinks. So each factor turns through $\frac{\theta}{n}$ times a number close to 1, the same number for every factor, and the $n$ factors turn through $\theta$ times that number in all, which comes to $\theta$ as $n$ grows. Each factor falls short by a small fraction of its own turn, so together they fall short by the same small fraction of $\theta$, and the shortfalls do not pile up. The question is whether $n$ stretches of a hair each add up to anything.

A thousand times as many steps leave a gap a thousand times smaller. So as $n$ grows, the length of $\left(1 + \frac{i\theta}{n}\right)^n$ settles on 1 and its angle on $\theta$: it settles on the point at angle $\theta$ on the circle of radius 1, and
$$e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta.$$
With $\theta = \pi$, half a turn from 1, $e^{i\pi} = -1$. So among the complex numbers $-1$ has a natural logarithm, a power of $e$ that gives it, $i\pi$, though M1.5 found none among the real numbers: `cmath.log(-1)` gives `3.141592653589793j`. 0 still has none.

M1.2's rule for powers holds here too. $e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta}$ turns through $\alpha$ and then through $\beta$, which is one turn through $\alpha + \beta$, so $e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta} = e^{i(\alpha + \beta)}$, as $b^{x + y} = b^x b^y$ says. Every complex number is its length times a turn, $re^{i\theta}$, so the polar form is often written that way, and multiplying $re^{i\theta}$ by $se^{i\phi}$ (phi) gives $rse^{i(\theta + \phi)}$: lengths multiply and angles add, in one line of exponent rules. In Python, `cmath.exp(1j * theta)` is $e^{i\theta}$, and `cmath.rect(r, theta)` is $re^{i\theta}$.

<Definition term="Euler's formula">

For every angle $\theta$, $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$, the point at angle $\theta$ on the circle of radius 1. It is where $\left(1 + \frac{i\theta}{n}\right)^n$ settles as $n$ grows, $n$ small turns that add up to $\theta$ and stretch by about $\frac{\theta^2}{2n}$ in all. So $e^{i\pi} = -1$, $e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta} = e^{i(\alpha + \beta)}$, and multiplying by $e^{i\theta}$ turns the plane through $\theta$ about 0. Python's `cmath.exp` gives it.
