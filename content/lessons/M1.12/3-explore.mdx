## The number i

No real number has a negative square: a positive number times itself is positive, and so is a negative number times itself. So invent one. Call it $i$, and ask only two things of it: that $i^2 = -1$, and that it obeys every rule of M1.2's algebra, so that it can be added, multiplied and multiplied out of brackets like any number. The numbers built from it, $a + bi$ for real numbers $a$ and $b$, are the *complex numbers*. $a$ is the *real part*, and $b$, the number that multiplies $i$, is the *imaginary part*. In Python, `1j` is $i$, `3 + 1j` is $3 + i$, and `complex(3, 1)` builds the same number from its two parts.

A complex number is a pair of real numbers, and so is a point of the plane. Draw $a + bi$ as the point $(a, b)$: the real numbers are the first axis, and $i$ is the point $(0, 1)$, a step up from 0. Adding works part by part, $(2 + i) + (1 + 3i) = 3 + 4i$, which is laying an arrow from 0 to $(1, 3)$ on the end of an arrow from 0 to $(2, 1)$. Multiplying is where something new happens. Multiply out the brackets as M1.2 does, and put $-1$ wherever $i^2$ turns up.

Draw it. $3 + i$ is the point $(3, 1)$, and $-1 + 3i$ is the point $(-1, 3)$, which is $(3, 1)$ turned a quarter turn anticlockwise about 0, by M1.11's rule that a quarter turn takes $(x, y)$ to $(-y, x)$. It works for every point: $i(a + bi) = ai + bi^2 = -b + ai$, which is $(-b, a)$. So multiplying by $i$ turns the plane a quarter turn about 0, and multiplying by $i$ twice turns it half a turn, which takes 1 to $-1$. That is all $i^2 = -1$ says.

René Descartes called these numbers *imaginary* in 1637, meaning it as a slight, and the name stuck. They are no more imaginary than the negative numbers, which Michael Stifel called absurd in 1544: they are the points of the plane, with a way of multiplying that turns.

## Square roots of negative numbers

$i^2 = -1$, and $(-i)^2 = (-1)^2 i^2 = -1$ as well, so $-1$ has two square roots, $i$ and $-i$, as 4 has 2 and $-2$. In the same way $(2i)^2 = 4i^2 = -4$, so $-4$ has the square roots $2i$ and $-2i$. M1.4's quadratic formula, which stopped at $\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}$ when $b^2 - 4ac$ was negative, can now go on: $x^2 + 4x + 5 = 0$ has $b^2 - 4ac = -4$, so $x = \frac{-4 \pm 2i}{2} = -2 \pm i$. Check one: $(-2 + i)^2 + 4(-2 + i) + 5 = (3 - 4i) + (-8 + 4i) + 5 = 0$. Every quadratic has two solutions among the complex numbers, or one solution given twice. Python's `cmath` module, a `math` for complex numbers that `import cmath` brings in as `import math` brings in `math`, has a square root that takes any number: `cmath.sqrt(-4)` gives `2j`.

## Length and conjugate

The *length* of $z = a + bi$, written $|z|$, is the distance of the point $(a, b)$ from 0, which is $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ by Pythagoras' theorem, and Python's `abs` gives it: `abs(3 + 4j)` is `5.0`. Mirroring $z$ in the first axis gives $a - bi$, the *conjugate* of $z$, written $\bar z$, and in Python `z.conjugate()`. A number times its conjugate is $(a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 - abi + abi - b^2i^2 = a^2 + b^2$, a real number, the square of the length: $z\bar z = |z|^2$.

## Length and angle

As M1.11 found for any point of the plane, a complex number can be given by its two parts, or by its length $r$ and the angle $\theta$ that turns 1, the point $(1, 0)$, towards it. Its two parts are then $r\cos\theta$ and $r\sin\theta$, so
$$z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta),$$
which is the *polar form* of $z$. In Python, `cmath.phase(z)` gives the angle, in radians from $-\pi$ to $\pi$ as `math.atan2` does, `cmath.polar(z)` gives the length and the angle together, and `cmath.rect(r, theta)` builds the number from them. So `cmath.phase(1j)` is `1.5707963267948966`, a quarter turn, and `cmath.polar(1 + 1j)` is `(1.4142135623730951, 0.7853981633974483)`: length $\sqrt{2}$ at angle $\frac{\pi}{4}$, which is $45°$. Going back, `cmath.rect(2, math.pi / 6)` is `(1.7320508075688774+0.9999999999999999j)`, which is $\sqrt{3} + i$ up to rounding.

## Interest paid more and more often

Turning is half of what this lesson is after. The other half starts with money. Jacob Bernoulli asked in 1683 what happens when interest is paid more and more often.

<Exercise id="explore-compound" />

</Reveal>

<Reveal for="explore-compound">

Python keeps $e$ as `math.e`, which prints `2.718281828459045`. Even a million payments fall short of it in the seventh figure: `(1 + 1/1000000) ** 1000000` gives `2.7182804690957534`.

Interest at another rate gives a power of $e$. A year at $x$ times 100%, paid in $n$ parts, gives $\left(1 + \frac{x}{n}\right)^n$. For a positive $x$, write $n = xm$: then $\left(1 + \frac{x}{n}\right)^n = \left(1 + \frac{1}{m}\right)^{xm} = \left(\left(1 + \frac{1}{m}\right)^m\right)^x$, by the rule that a power of a power multiplies the exponents, which M1.5 used for every power. As $n$ grows so does $m$. It need not be a whole number, but $\left(1 + \frac{1}{m}\right)^m$ grows steadily with $m$, as the payments did, so it is caught between its values at the whole numbers either side of $m$, and settles on $e$ too. A number close to $e$, raised to the power $x$, is close to $e^x$, so the whole settles on $e^x$. A negative $x$, a year of losses, takes another step to show, and settles the same way: `(1 - 1/1000000) ** 1000000` gives `0.36787925722106646`, and $e^{-1} = 0.3678794\ldots$. So for every real number $x$, $e^x$ is where $\left(1 + \frac{x}{n}\right)^n$ settles as $n$ grows.

As $n$ grows, $\left(1 + \frac{i\theta}{n}\right)^n$ settles on the point at angle $\theta$ on the circle of radius 1. For a real $x$, where $\left(1 + \frac{x}{n}\right)^n$ settles is M1.5's power $e^x$. For $x = i\theta$ there is no power to agree with, since "$e$ multiplied by itself $i\theta$ times" means nothing, so where $\left(1 + \frac{i\theta}{n}\right)^n$ settles is taken as what $e^{i\theta}$ means:
$$e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta.$$
This is *Euler's formula*, which Leonhard Euler published in 1748. It earns the name of a power because M1.2's rule for powers still holds for it, $e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta} = e^{i(\alpha + \beta)}$, as the Derive phase shows. At $\theta = \pi$, half a turn, it says that $e^{i\pi} = -1$. Python's `cmath.exp` gives these powers of $e$: `cmath.exp(1j * math.pi)` gives `(-1+1.2246467991473532e-16j)`, which is $-1$ apart from M1.11's gap between `math.pi` and $\pi$. The Derive phase shows why the end of the chain comes to length 1 and to angle $\theta$.

## The names

<Definition term="Complex numbers">

The numbers $a + bi$, with $a$ and $b$ real and $i^2 = -1$, drawn as the points $(a, b)$ of the plane: $a$ is the real part and $b$ the imaginary part. They add part by part, and multiply by multiplying out brackets with $-1$ put for $i^2$; multiplying by $i$ turns the plane a quarter turn about 0. The length $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ and the conjugate $\bar z = a - bi$ have $z\bar z = |z|^2$. Every quadratic has two solutions among them. Python has `1j`, `complex(a, b)`, `z.real`, `z.imag`, `abs(z)`, `z.conjugate()` and `cmath.sqrt`.

</Definition>

<Definition term="Polar form">

A complex number of length $r$ at angle $\theta$ is $r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$. Multiplying two complex numbers multiplies their lengths and adds their angles, so $z^n$ has length $r^n$ and angle $n\theta$. Python's `cmath.phase`, `cmath.polar` and `cmath.rect` move between the two forms.

</Definition>

<Definition term="The number e">

$e = 2.718281828\ldots$ is where $\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n$, a year's interest of 100% paid in $n$ parts, settles as $n$ grows, and for any real number $x$, $e^x$ is where $\left(1 + \frac{x}{n}\right)^n$ settles; for an imaginary $x = i\theta$, that is what $e^{i\theta}$ is taken to mean. Python keeps $e$ as `math.e`, and `cmath.exp(1j * theta)` gives $e^{i\theta}$.

</Definition>
