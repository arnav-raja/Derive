## Formulas into Python

On paper, a formula's layout does some of the grouping for you. A fraction bar groups everything above it and everything below it, and an exponent groups everything raised up. In Python a formula is one line of text, so each such group needs brackets of its own.

Powers can be fractions too, settled as M1.2 settled $a^0$: by keeping a rule true. If $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$ is to hold, then $(v^{1/2})^2 = v^1 = v$, so $v^{1/2}$ is the number whose square is $v$, its square root, and $\sqrt{v}$ is `v ** 0.5`. In the same way $27^{1/3}$ is the cube root of 27, since $(27^{1/3})^3 = 27$.

When Python cannot carry out a line, it stops and reports an error: its kind, such as the `TypeError` that Explore met, what went wrong, and the line it stopped at. The lines before it have run; the lines after it have not.

## Ask Python for its tree

Python's `ast` module, short for *abstract syntax tree*, shows the tree that Python builds for an expression. This program

```python
import ast
print(ast.dump(ast.parse("1 + 2 * 3", mode="eval").body, indent=2))
```

prints the tree one piece to a line, each piece indented under the one it belongs to:

```
BinOp(
  left=Constant(value=1),
  op=Add(),
  right=BinOp(
    left=Constant(value=2),
    op=Mult(),
    right=Constant(value=3)))
```

Read it from the outside in. `BinOp` is an operation with two sides, `left` and `right`, and `op` names it: `Add`, `Sub`, `Mult`, `Div`, `FloorDiv`, `Mod` or `Pow`. `UnaryOp` with `op=USub()` is a minus sign in front, and `Constant` is a number. So the top of this tree is the addition, with the tree for `2 * 3` below it on the right, just as M1.2 drew it.

## Comparing floats

Two floats that should be equal may differ in their last digits, so a program should ask whether two floats are *close enough*, within a tolerance, rather than whether they are exactly equal. `abs(a - b)` is the distance between `a` and `b`, and `abs(a - b) < 0.5` is `True` when `a` and `b` are less than 0.5 apart: `<`, `>`, `<=` and `>=` compare as in maths, and give `True` or `False`, as `==` does. The question is how close is close enough.

A tolerance must be wide enough to forgive rounding, which near 1 is about $10^{-16}$, and narrow enough to catch a real mistake, here $10^{-4}$. `math.isclose(a, b)`, after `import math` at the top of the program, is Python's standard check: it is `True` when `a` and `b` differ by at most a billionth of the larger of the two, so they agree to about 9 significant figures. Rounding with `round(total, 2) == 1`, or allowing a difference of 0.01, would have let the mistake through, and so would `total - 1 < 1e-9` without the `abs`, since a total below 1, even 0.5, gives a negative difference. Reordering the sum until `==` happens to say `True` is worse still, since the next sum will not be so lucky.
