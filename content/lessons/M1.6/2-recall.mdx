This lesson builds on M1.2's rules for numbers and for solving equations, and on M1.3's functions. Five quick questions check that they are ready. Answer from memory, without a calculator. A miss opens a short re-teach straight away.

<Callback lesson="M1.2">

Distributivity says $a(b + c) = ab + ac$. Read from right to left, it takes a factor that every term shares out of a sum: $5 \times 8 + 5 \times 2 = 5 \times (8 + 2) = 5 \times 10$. This lesson uses it on long sums, where every term has the same factor.

</Callback>

<Exercise id="recall-regroup" />

<Reteach for="recall-regroup">

<Callback lesson="M1.2">

Associativity lets brackets move, $(x + y) + z = x + (y + z)$, and commutativity lets two terms swap, $x + y = y + x$. With enough of these moves, the terms of a sum can be put in any order and grouped in any way. $(a + b) + (c + d)$ and $(a + d) + (b + c)$ add the same four numbers, so they are equal for all numbers. On a computer, rounding can make the two differ slightly, as M1.2 showed, which is why the question said "exactly".

</Callback>

</Reteach>

<Reteach for="recall-power-up">

<Callback lesson="M1.2">

$r^6$ is six copies of $r$ multiplied together, and one more copy makes seven: $r \times r^6 = r^{1 + 6} = r^7$. Multiplying powers of the same number adds the exponents, $a^m \times a^n = a^{m + n}$, and $r$ on its own is $r^1$.

</Callback>

</Reteach>

<Exercise id="recall-solve" />

<Reteach for="recall-solve">

<Callback lesson="M1.2">

Collect the terms in $S$ first: by distributivity, $S - 0.9S = (1 - 0.9)S = 0.1S$. Then do the same to both sides to leave $S$ on its own: dividing both sides of $0.1S = 2$ by 0.1 gives $S = 20$. Check it: $20 - 0.9 \times 20 = 20 - 18 = 2$.

</Callback>

</Reteach>

<Reteach for="recall-def">

<Callback lesson="M1.3">

In Python a function is written with `def`: the name in brackets stands for its input, and `return` gives the output. `f(4)` runs the body with `x` standing for 4, so it returns $3 \times 4 + 1 = 13$. A function can take more than one input, separated by commas, as `geometric(r, n)` does in Build.

</Callback>
