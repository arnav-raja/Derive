## Whole numbers and decimals

Python keeps two kinds of number. A whole number such as `10` is an *int*, and Python stores it exactly, however many digits it has. A number written with a decimal point, such as `10.0`, is a *float*, stored to about 16 significant figures, as M1.2 showed. Dividing with `/` always gives a float, so `10 / 2` is `5.0`. Dividing with `//` divides and then rounds down to a whole number, so it keeps whole numbers whole: `10 // 2` is `5`, and `7 // 2` is `3`.

## Python's Σ

Python has a short form for a sum, much like $\Sigma$. `range(a, b)` counts the whole numbers from `a` up to, but not including, `b`: `range(1, 5)` counts 1, 2, 3 and 4. Given a single number, `range(5)` counts from 0, giving 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4. Then

```python
print(sum(k * k for k in range(1, 5)))   # prints 30
```

works out `k * k` for each `k` that `range` counts, and adds the results. It is $\sum_{k=1}^{4} k^2 = 1 + 4 + 9 + 16$. Read the `for` as $\Sigma$'s counter and `range` as its two ends, with the top end left out.

A slip of one at the end of a range is common enough to have a name, a *fencepost error*: a fence 10 metres long with a post every metre needs 11 posts, not 10. The quickest check of a range's ends is the smallest case, here a single term, which must be 1.

## The formula's blind spot

`geometric` adds the terms one by one. Derive's formula finds the same sum with one division.

A formula and the sum it stands for can agree everywhere except where the formula divides by 0. `geometric`, which adds the terms, has no such gap, and `geometric(1, 10)` gives `10`. Code that uses the formula must treat a ratio of 1 on its own.

## Compare with the library

SymPy, the algebra library first met in M1.1, can find sums and products with letters in them. `summation(expression, (k, a, b))` is $\sum_{k=a}^{b}$ of the expression, with both ends included, unlike `range`, and `product` does the same for $\Pi$. SymPy's infinity is `oo`, two letter o's, for a sum with no last term. The starter finds $\sum_{k=1}^{n} k^2$ as an example.

Its geometric sum is a `Piecewise`, an answer in cases: $n$ when $r = 1$, and $\frac{1 - r^n}{1 - r}$ otherwise, which is exactly the blind spot that the break-it found. Its sum with no last term holds only when $|r| < 1$, that is, for $r$ between $-1$ and 1, and for any other $r$ SymPy leaves the sum as it is, since then it has no finite total. `squares`, $\frac{n^3}{3} + \frac{n^2}{2} + \frac{n}{6}$, is a formula for $1^2 + 2^2 + \dots + n^2$ that pairing cannot find, and M1.9's induction can prove.
