## The sum of the squares

In M1.6, SymPy gave the sum of the first $n$ squares as $\frac{n^3}{3} + \frac{n^2}{2} + \frac{n}{6}$, a formula that pairing cannot find. Over a common denominator it is $\frac{2n^3 + 3n^2 + n}{6}$, and the top is $n(n + 1)(2n + 1)$, as multiplying out shows. At $n = 3$ the formula gives $\frac{3 \times 4 \times 7}{6} = 14$, which is $1 + 4 + 9$. That is evidence, and induction makes it a proof.

## Why induction works

The Explore phase trusted the chain: the base case, then step after step, reaching every whole number. But nobody can take infinitely many steps. Here is a proof that the base case and the step do cover every whole number. It is a proof by contradiction, and it rests on one fact about the whole numbers.

<Stated>

Every collection of whole numbers with at least one member has a smallest member.

</Stated>

It is not proved here, for a reason: a proof of it needs induction, so one of the two has to be taken as a starting point, a fact that says what the whole numbers are, and this lesson takes the smallest member. Fractions have no such fact: the fractions greater than 0 have no smallest, since half of any one of them is smaller.

The same proof shows that strong induction works, since every case below the smallest counterexample would be true, and the strong step carries them all up to it. It shows, too, why E1.2's invariants hold after every pass: the first pass to break an invariant would have to follow a pass that kept it, and every pass keeps it.

## Counting the calls of `fib`

E1.5 counted the calls that the plain `fib(n)` makes, the first call included, as $C(n)$: $C(0) = C(1) = 1$, and $C(n) = 1 + C(n - 1) + C(n - 2)$ for $n \ge 2$. Write $F(n)$ for the Fibonacci number that `fib(n)` gives, so that $F(0) = 0$, $F(1) = 1$, and $F(n) = F(n - 1) + F(n - 2)$ for $n \ge 2$. Side by side:

| $n$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $C(n)$ | 1 | 1 | 3 | 5 | 9 | 15 | 25 | 41 |
| $F(n + 1)$ | 1 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 8 | 13 | 21 |

Each count is one less than twice the Fibonacci number below it, which suggests that $C(n) = 2F(n + 1) - 1$ for every $n \ge 0$. The step for $C(k + 1)$ needs both $C(k)$ and $C(k - 1)$, so this is a strong induction, with two base cases: $C(0) = 1 = 2F(1) - 1$ and $C(1) = 1 = 2F(2) - 1$.

## The running average, by induction

M1.6 smoothed gradients with a running average, $m_t = \beta m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta) g_t$ with $m_0 = 0$, and found by summing a geometric series that when every gradient is the same number $g$, $m_t = (1 - \beta^t) g$. Induction proves it again, in a few lines, from the recurrence alone. The base case holds, since $\beta^0 = 1$: $m_0 = 0 = (1 - \beta^0) g$.

With the base case, that proves $m_t = (1 - \beta^t) g$ for every $t \ge 0$, as M1.6 found by summing.

Induction also proves bounds that every step keeps. Suppose that an optimiser's distance from the best setting, $d_t$, shrinks by at least a tenth at every step: $d_{t+1} \le 0.9\,d_t$. Then $d_t \le 0.9^t d_0$ for every $t \ge 0$. Base case: $d_0 \le 0.9^0 d_0 = d_0$. Step: if $d_k \le 0.9^k d_0$, then $d_{k+1} \le 0.9\,d_k \le 0.9 \times 0.9^k d_0 = 0.9^{k+1} d_0$, where the second inequality multiplies the hypothesis by 0.9, a positive number, which keeps it. After 50 steps, $0.9^{50}$ is about 0.005, so the distance is at most about half a per cent of where it started. M10.4 proves bounds of this kind for gradient descent.

## A power above a line

The last proof is yours. Besides adding the same number to both sides, it needs one more fact about inequalities. Multiplying both sides of $a \ge b$ by a number $c \ge 0$ keeps it, $ac \ge bc$, since $ac - bc = (a - b)c$ is a product of two numbers that are each at least 0. Multiplying by a negative number turns it round: $3 \ge 2$, but $3 \times (-1) < 2 \times (-1)$.

This is *Bernoulli's inequality*, named after Jacob Bernoulli, who published it in 1689. It puts a simple line under a power: $0.999^{100} \ge 1 - 100 \times 0.001 = 0.9$, so a hundred steps that each keep 99.9 per cent of something keep at least 90 per cent of it, and about 90.5 per cent in fact.
